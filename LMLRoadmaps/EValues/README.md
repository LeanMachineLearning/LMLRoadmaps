# Roadmap: E-values, e-processes and anytime-valid inference

Develop hypothesis testing with e-values: nonnegative statistics whose expectation is at most one
under every distribution of the null hypothesis. Their sequential counterparts, e-processes, stay
valid at every stopping time, so that a statistician may monitor the data, stop or continue at
will, and report a level chosen after seeing the data. The development follows A. Ramdas and
R. Wang, *Hypothesis Testing with E-values* [RW], and the papers it surveys and that followed it.

The literature is recent and its results supersede one another quickly. Each layer therefore
states the strongest result available, often from a 2025–2026 paper or from Statlib's general
theory of the numeraire, and derives the earlier and narrower results from it; those are listed
as corollaries, never as separate targets. The headline results are:
- the characterization of e-processes as the processes dominated by a test supermartingale for
  each null distribution [RRLK, Lemma 6], with Ville's inequality from the exact Ville identity
  [dlP+26], and the composite Ville theorem [RLKR23];
- the identification of numeraires through the effective null [LRR25, Theorem 4.1], which contains
  the log-optimality of likelihood ratios at every σ-algebra and every stopping time;
- the e-variables of nulls generated by finitely many constraints [LRR26, Theorem 3.1], their
  admissible bets, which form a minimal complete class with no constraint qualification, and the
  dual formula $\mathrm{KL}_{\inf}=\sup_\pi\mathbb E_Q\log(1+\sum_i\pi_ig_i)$ for every such null
  on any measurable space, with no constraint qualification [EVR26], of which the bounded,
  semi-bounded and heavy-tailed $\mathrm K_{\inf}$ formulas of the bandit literature are instances;
- the optimal growth rate `d` of e-processes against an i.i.d. composite null, the limit of the
  normalized maximal expected logarithm of `n`-sample e-variables [RR26]: it bounds the growth of
  every e-process almost surely, is the exponent of the Stein lemma for composite nulls, and gives
  the sharp bound $\mathbb E_Q[\tau_\alpha]\ge\log(1/\alpha)/d$ on the level-`α` sequential tests
  [EVR26]; it equals $\mathrm{KL}_{\inf}$ under lower semicontinuity [RR26], which gives the bound
  of [ARR26]; and the existence of power-one tests against composite alternatives is characterized
  by finite-block events [Ram26];
- the Wald inequality with a design: for data that an algorithm collects from several sources,
  the expected logarithm of every e-variable at a stopping time is at most the rate `D*` of the
  designs times the expected number of observations, so that $\mathbb E[\tau]\ge
  \mathrm{kl}(\delta,1-\delta)/D^*$ for every `δ`-correct identification algorithm, with
  `D* ≤ 1 / T*` and an algorithm that learns the design and attains `D*` [EVR26];
- testing by betting: against a conditional mean constrained to a convex set, every e-process is
  dominated by a betting wealth [Cle26b]; and confidence sequences by duality;
- merging: the e-merging functions are the functions dominated by weighted means [Wan25; Cle26],
  and the admissible merging functions of sequential e-values are the martingale merging
  functions [VW24];
- multiple testing through dynamic e-closure [dH26], of which fixed-time e-closure [XSF+26], e-BH
  for compound e-values, closed e-BH, closed BH [Goe26], online e-BH and stopped e-BH are
  instances;
- exchangeability, conformal e-prediction, change detection and nonparametric two-sample testing.

Build the surrounding library at the generality of the literature:
- e-variables, e-processes and p-variables for arbitrary sets of measures, with no dominating
  measure, no convexity and no parametrization, valued in `ℝ≥0∞`;
- the polarity between nulls and bets, of which the effective null is the closure, and the
  operations on nulls (images, products, i.i.d. and independent sequences, conditioning, stopping)
  as definitions;
- any filtration indexed by `ℕ`, and data that need not be independent;
- conditional e-variables and test supermartingales stated with set integrals, with no
  integrability side conditions;
- Lagrange duality for finitely many constraints, the essential supremum of a family of functions,
  and the discrete-time Snell envelope.

## Scope and dependencies

This roadmap owns e-values and e-processes: their definitions for composite nulls, their
validity, optimality and growth, their merging, and their use in sequential testing, confidence
sequences, multiple testing, conformal prediction and change detection. It also owns four general
tools that it needs and that no dependency provides: Lagrange duality for convex programs with
finitely many constraints (Layer 1.4), the convergence and optional stopping of `ℝ≥0∞`-valued
supermartingales at arbitrary stopping times (Layer 2.2), the essential supremum of a family of
functions with the discrete-time Snell envelope (Layer 3.1), and first-passage times of random
walks with positive drift (Layer 6.3). It owns the dual formula of $\mathrm K_{\inf}$ for
nonparametric classes (Layer 1.4), which the Identification roadmap's Layer 1.4 defines and does not
compute, and the Wald inequality with a design (Layer 6.4), which the Identification roadmap's
Layers 2.3 and 5.4 and the Bandits roadmap's Layer 4.4 use.

**Statlib.** Statlib, a dependency of LML at LMLExplore's pin, already contains the theory of
e-variables at a fixed sample size: definitions, convexity, the existence of the numeraire for any
class of e-variables (`IsEVarClass`) and any utility, the reverse information projection and its
duality with the Kullback–Leibler divergence for any set of measures, products, and Bernoulli
computations. This roadmap consumes them, does not restate them, and builds on them: the
log-optimality of likelihood ratios, the dual formula of $\mathrm{KL}_{\inf}$ and the optimal
growth rate of e-processes are all derived from Statlib's numeraire and duality.

**Outside this roadmap:**

  Ville's inequality for càdlàg supermartingales;
- asymptotic and approximate e-values and asymptotic confidence sequences ([RW, Chapter 10];
  [WSA+24]), which rest on strong approximation;
- e-values and risk measures [RW, Chapter 16], merging of dependent p-values [RW, Chapter 12], and
  incentive-compatible contracts [RW, §4.4];
- composite alternatives: GROW over finitely additive closures [RLRR26] and the robust growth

- the optimality of the sequential probability ratio test [WW48] and the boosted SPRT;
- knockoffs, and the complete classes of FDR procedures with e-values;
- confidence sequences for heavy-tailed, Banach-valued or vector-valued data, PAC-Bayes
  confidence sequences and Huber-robust confidence sequences.

## Conventions

**Sources and numbering.** Statements cite [RW] by the numbering of its sixth arXiv version, which
the fifth shares (the fourth differs), and the original papers by the keys of the References
section with the numbering of the arXiv version listed there. The results cited [EVR26] have no
other source: the layers state them in full, and cite the note of [EVR26] that proves them. Rounds
are numbered from 0.

**Nulls.** A null hypothesis is a set `S : Set (Measure Ω)`, as for Statlib's `IsEVar`; formulas
write it $\mathcal P$ (Mathlib reserves `𝒫` for the power set). A hypothesis on its members, that
they are probability measures or have mass at most one, appears only where the constant `1` is a
bet or a member is normalized.
- No dominating measure, no convexity and no topology are assumed unless the statement names one
  (likelihood ratios, universal inference, the dominated forms of Layer 1, the weak topology of
  Layer 6.1).
- A parametric null is `Set.range P` for `P : Θ → Measure Ω`.
- A family of nulls indexed by parameters, `S : Θ → Set (Measure Ω)` with `P ∈ S θ` meaning that
  `θ` is a true parameter of `P`, is the input of confidence sequences (Layer 5). A functional
  `φ` on a model `M` is the case `S θ = {P ∈ M | φ P = θ}`; partially identified parameters are
  covered.
- Statements about a composite null hold under each of its members: "almost surely" means
  `P`-almost surely for every `P ∈ S`, and independence is independence under every `P ∈ S`. A
  property holds `S`-quasi-surely when it holds `P`-almost surely for every `P ∈ S`; statements
  write it with the filter `quasiSure S` of Layer 0.6, and `=ᶠ`, `≤ᶠ` for it.
- Nulls are built with the named operations of Layer 0.6 (`mapNull`, `sampleNull`, `iidNull`,
  `indepNull`), of Layers 2.1, 3.1 and 4.5 (`condNull`, `stoppedNull`, `sequentialNull`) and of
  Layers 0.1 and 7 (`superUniformNull`, `mergingNull`). No statement writes one of these images
  inline.
- Validity for the largest null, growth for the smallest. A statement of validity (a process is a
  test supermartingale or an e-process, a function is an e-variable) is made for the largest null of
  the chain `iidNull S₁ ⊆ indepNull S₁ ⊆ sequentialNull (fun _ _ ↦ S₁) X ℱ` for which it holds, and
  the smaller nulls are instances; an upper bound on growth is made for the smallest. A one-step
  null may depend on the round and on the past (Layer 4.5).
- The e-variables of a null are written `{X | IsEVar X S}`, with no name of their own. A null
  presented by bets `𝓔` is `polarNull 𝓔` (Layer 0.6), intersected with the probability measures
  where needed, and a complete class theorem describes `{X | IsEVar X (polarNull 𝓔)}` from `𝓔`.
- A confidence set, a confidence sequence and an e-collection are stated from a family `E θ` of
  e-variables or e-processes for the nulls `S θ` of the laws of which `θ` is true; the truth may be
  a parameter, a set of parameters or a set of true hypotheses.

**Values.** E-variables, e-processes, test supermartingales, wealth processes and merging
functions are `ℝ≥0∞`-valued, and expectations are lower integrals `∫⁻`.
- Statlib's `IsEVar X S` is `Measurable X ∧ ∀ μ ∈ S, ∫⁻ X ∂μ ≤ 1` (its fields `measurable` and
  `lintegral_le_one`), for every set of measures: the pairing of the bipolar theorem [BS99], under
  which the effective null is the polar of the set of e-variables. The constant `1` is an
  e-variable exactly when every member has mass at most one (`isEVar_one_iff`).
- A level-`α` test is a measurable `φ` with values in `[0, 1]` and `∫⁻ φ ∂P ≤ α` on the null
  (`IsTest φ S α`, Layer 0.1). Its indicator form and its form "`α` times an e-variable with values
  at most `α⁻¹`" are lemmas.
- A map that preserves validity (a calibrator, an adjuster, a merging function, a combiner) is
  characterized as an e-variable for a null on the space of values (`superUniformNull`,
  `mergingNull 𝒞`), and its theory is derived from the theory of e-variables.
- P-variables are `ℝ≥0∞`-valued as well, with `P {ω | p ω ≤ α} ≤ α` for every `α : ℝ≥0∞`; values
  above `1` carry no evidence. The e-to-p calibration is `E ↦ E⁻¹`, with `0⁻¹ = ∞` and
  `∞⁻¹ = 0`, and no truncation at `1`.
- Levels are `α : ℝ≥0∞`; an e-test rejects when `α⁻¹ ≤ E`.
- Real nonnegative supermartingales (Mathlib's `Supermartingale`) enter through `ENNReal.ofReal`
  (Layer 2.1).
- Expected logarithms of `ℝ≥0∞`-valued variables are `∫ᵉ ω, ENNReal.log (X ω) ∂Q`, valued in
  `EReal`, as in Statlib's `maxUtility` with `logUtility` and `IsNumeraire.eintegral_log_le`;
  never the Bochner integral, which is `0` for non-integrable functions. The largest expected
  logarithm of an e-variable is `maxEPower Q S := maxUtility Q S logUtility`, and the smallest
  relative entropy to a null is `klInf Q S := ⨅ P ∈ S, klIntegral Q P` in `EReal`, with Statlib's
  `klIntegral Q P = ∫ log (dQ/dP) dQ` (Layer 1.1). For probability measures `klIntegral` is
  Mathlib's `klDiv`; on the sub-probability measures of an effective null, or of a closure of a
  null, `klDiv` adds the difference of the masses and `klIntegral` is the divergence that the
  theory produces.

**Conditional statements.** Conditional expectations of `ℝ≥0∞`-valued variables are not used.
"$\mathbb E_P[X\mid m]\le Y$" is stated as
`∀ s, MeasurableSet[m] s → ∫⁻ ω in s, X ω ∂P ≤ ∫⁻ ω in s, Y ω ∂P`, the form of LMLExplore's
`supermartingale_of_setLIntegral_succ_le`. A conditional law is given by a kernel through the same
set-integral identity, or Mathlib's `HasCondDistrib`.

**Time, filtrations and stopping times.**
- Time is `ℕ` and filtrations are arbitrary: `ℱ : Filtration ℕ mΩ`. Observations need not be
  independent, identically distributed or a product. The canonical filtration of a sequence of
  observations on `ℕ → 𝓧` is `Filtration.piLT` (Layer 6.1), the observations before `n`, so that
  time `n` has seen `n` observations; `piLT (n + 1) = piLE n` relates it to Mathlib's
  `Filtration.piLE` and to Statlib's `Testing/Sequential/`. Statements hold for every filtration.
- A process `E` is adapted when `E n` is `ℱ n`-measurable. The factor of round `n` of a product, a
  bet or a sequential e-variable, is `ℱ (n + 1)`-measurable, and is a conditional e-variable given
  `ℱ n`; bets and portfolios used in round `n` are `ℱ n`-measurable.
- Stopping times are Mathlib's `IsStoppingTime ℱ τ` with `τ : Ω → WithTop ℕ`. A stopping time with
  values in `ℕ` is the coercion `fun ω ↦ (τ ω : WithTop ℕ)`, as in Statlib's
  `Testing/Sequential/`. Mathlib's `stoppedValue` takes an arbitrary value at `⊤`; never rely on
  it. The value of a process at a possibly infinite stopping time is `stoppedValueLimsup E τ`,
  equal to `E (τ ω) ω` where `τ ω` is finite and to `limsup (E · ω)` where it is infinite
  [RRLK; RLKR23, (1.2)].
- A level-`α` sequential test is a stopping time `τ` with `P {τ < ⊤} ≤ α` for every member of the
  null (Layer 3.3); its power against `Q` is `Q {τ < ⊤}`.

**Adaptively collected data.** Statements about data that an algorithm collects (Layer 6.4) are made
for any algorithm–environment pair of LML (`IsAlgEnvSeq`), with arms of a finite type `α`,
observations in any measurable space `𝓨` (not real rewards), randomized algorithms, and a stopping
time of the filtration of the history. An instance is a family of arm laws `α → Measure 𝓨`, and a
set of instances is any `Λ : Set (α → Measure 𝓨)`; no product structure is assumed. "Attains `c` at
`μ`" means `limsup_{δ → 0} E_μ[τ_δ] / log (1 / δ) ≤ c` for one family of algorithms indexed by `δ`,
with no uniformity over instances unless a statement says so.

**Theorem shapes.** Validity statements quantify over every member of the null and every stopping
time. Constants are explicit. Asymptotic statements are eventual inequalities or limits along
`atTop` and `𝓝[>] 0`, as in the Identification roadmap.

**Generality.** Each layer says where its statements generalize the papers. Do not reintroduce
simple nulls, dominated nulls, real-valued e-values, i.i.d. data, bounded observations or finite
horizons as the primary statements where the layer states the general form, and do not prove a
special case listed as a corollary before the general statement: derive it.

**Namespaces and names.** Definitions live in `ProbabilityTheory`, next to Statlib's, with names
in Statlib's pattern: `IsPVar`, `IsCondEVar`, `IsTestSupermartingale`, `IsTestMartingale`,
`IsEProcess`, `IsPProcess`, `IsConfSeq`, `IsEMerging`, `IsCompoundEVar`, `IsECollection`,
`IsTest`, `constraintNull`, `polarNull`, `mapNull`, `quasiSure`, `klInf`, `maxEPower`. Statlib's
names keep their names.

## Layer 0: E-variables, p-variables, calibration, post-hoc validity, nulls and bets

1. **P-variables, tests and Markov's inequality** [RW, Definition 1.2, Fact 1.3, Proposition 2.1].
   - Define `IsPVar p S` for a measurable `p : Ω → ℝ≥0∞`: `P {ω | p ω ≤ α} ≤ α` for every
     `P ∈ S` and every `α : ℝ≥0∞`. Define exact e-variables (`∫⁻ E ∂P = 1` for every `P ∈ S`)
     and exact p-variables (`P {p ≤ α} = α` for `α ≤ 1`).
   - Define `IsTest φ S α` for `φ : Ω → ℝ≥0∞`: `φ` is measurable, `φ ≤ 1`, and `∫⁻ φ ∂P ≤ α` for

   - Prove Markov's inequality for e-variables, `P {ω | α⁻¹ ≤ E ω} ≤ α`, for every null, hence that

   - Define the super-uniform null `superUniformNull`, the probability measures `μ` on `ℝ≥0∞` with

   - Prove that products of finitely many e-variables that are independent under every `P ∈ S`

2. **Calibrators** [RW, Definition 2.3, Propositions 2.4–2.7; VW21, Propositions 2.1–2.2].
   - Prove that a measurable `f : ℝ≥0∞ → ℝ≥0∞` is an e-variable for `superUniformNull` exactly when
     its antitone envelope `u ↦ ⨆ q ≥ u, f q` has integral at most one on `[0, 1]`. A super-uniform
     law dominates the uniform law stochastically, which gives one direction; for the other, the
     law of `q (U)` for a selection `q u ≥ u` that nearly attains the envelope on the cells of a
     grid is super-uniform. The p-to-e calibrators are then the antitone e-variables for

   - Define a p-to-e calibrator as an antitone `f : ℝ≥0∞ → ℝ≥0∞`, zero on `(1, ∞]`, with
     `∫⁻ x in Icc 0 1, f (ENNReal.ofReal x) ≤ 1`: [RW, Proposition 2.5]'s characterization, a case
     of the previous bullet, is the definition.
     Prove that `f ∘ p` is an e-variable for every p-variable `p` for every null on
     every measurable space, and conversely that an antitone `f` vanishing on `(1, ∞]` for which
     `f` applied to the identity of `[0, 1]` under Lebesgue measure is an e-variable is a
     calibrator.
   - Prove the admissibility characterization (upper semicontinuous, `f 0 = ∞`, integral `1`),
     the rescaling `x ↦ a f (a x)` for `a ≥ 1` [RW, Proposition 2.6], and the instances
     $\kappa x^{\kappa-1}$, $\kappa\in(0,1)$, and their mixture over $\kappa$, with its closed
     form $(1-x+x\log x)/(x(\log x)^2)$ on $(0,1)$.
   - E-to-p: every antitone `g` such that `g ∘ E` is a p-variable for every e-variable `E` on the
     unit interval satisfies `min 1 t⁻¹ ≤ g t`, so `t ↦ min 1 t⁻¹` is the only admissible e-to-p
     calibrator [RW, Proposition 2.4].
   - For an atomless probability measure `P`, every e-variable for `{P}` is `P`-almost surely
     `f ∘ p` for a calibrator `f` and an exact p-variable `p`, with `f` admissible when the
     e-variable is exact [RW, Proposition 2.7].
3. **The randomized Markov inequality** [RW, Theorem 2.9, Corollary 2.10; RM26, Theorem 1.2].
   For a measurable `Y : Ω → ℝ≥0∞` and `U` uniform on `[0, 1]` and independent of `Y` under a
   probability measure `P`, prove `P {U ≤ α * Y} = ∫⁻ min (α * Y) 1 ∂P ≤ α * ∫⁻ Y ∂P`. Deduce

   under every member of the null, and that `U / E` is one.
4. **Post-hoc validity** [RW, Definition 4.1, Propositions 4.3–4.4].
   - For a family of tests `φ : ℝ≥0∞ → Ω → ℝ≥0∞` indexed by the levels in `(0, 1)`, with values in
     `[0, 1]`, define post-hoc validity: `∫⁻ ω, ⨆ α ∈ Ioo 0 1, φ α ω / α ∂P ≤ 1` for every `P ∈ S`.
     Prove its equivalence with `∫⁻ φ (α̂ ω) ω / α̂ ω ∂P ≤ 1` for every measurable random level
     `α̂ : Ω → Ioo 0 1`, for families nondecreasing in the level, for which the supremum is over
     the rational levels and no measurable selection is needed.
   - Prove that `fun α ω ↦ min (α * E ω) 1` and the indicators of `{α⁻¹ ≤ E}` are post-hoc valid
     for every e-variable `E`, and that every post-hoc valid family nondecreasing in the level is
     dominated by `min (α * E) 1`, with `E = ⨆ α, φ α / α` an e-variable [RW, Proposition 4.3].
   - Corollary: `p` is a post-hoc p-variable (`∫⁻ ⨆ α ∈ Ioo 0 1, 𝟙{p ≤ α} / α ≤ 1`) exactly when
     `p⁻¹`, truncated to `0` on `{1 ≤ p}`, is an e-variable [RW, Proposition 4.4], which is
     [Kon23, Theorem 2] and the e-variable compatibility of [Gru24, Proposition 1].
5. **Admissibility** [RW, Definition 2.11, Theorem 2.14, Corollary 2.15, Propositions 2.12, 2.13
   and 2.17; LRR26, Lemma 6.2; EVR26, Notes 42, 45 and 46].
   - Define admissibility once, as maximality for the order `≤ᶠ[quasiSure S]` (item 6) on a set of
     bets, and
     use it for e-variables, calibrators, merging functions and e-processes (Layers 0.2, 3.7, 7).
     An admissible e-variable is one that every e-variable `E' ≥ E` equals quasi-surely. Prove once
     that a complete class whose members are admissible is the set of admissible elements, up to
     quasi-sure versions [LRR26, Lemma 6.2].
   - Prove the characterization of [RW, Theorem 2.14]: `E` is admissible exactly when
     $\liminf_{\delta\downarrow0}\inf\{(1-\mathbb E_P[E])/\delta : P\in\mathcal P,\ P(A)>\delta\}=0$
     for every measurable `A`, with `⨅ ∅ = 0`. Derive from it its sufficient conditions [RW, Corollary 2.15], the necessary
     condition [RW, Proposition 2.13], and for a simple null `{P}` that admissible and exact
     coincide, with every exact e-variable `P`-almost surely `S.rnDeriv P` for a probability
     measure `S ≪ P` [RW, Proposition 2.12].
   - Call `E` strongly admissible when every measurable `A` charged by some member of the null is
     charged by some `P ∈ S` with `∫⁻ E ∂P = 1` [RW, Corollary 2.15(a)]. Products of strongly
     admissible e-variables are strongly admissible for the product null [RW, Proposition 2.17].
   - For any null with no domination, the criterion of [RW, Theorem 2.14] in the form with a ratio:
     `E` is admissible exactly when, for every set `B` that is not polar,
     `⨅ {(1 - ∫⁻ E ∂P) / P B | P ∈ S, 0 < P B} = 0` [EVR26, Note 46].
   - An e-variable is a numeraire against some probability measure exactly when it is exact under a
     member of the effective null, or infinite on a nonempty set; a numeraire against an alternative
     that dominates the null is admissible; for a finite null an e-variable is admissible exactly
     when the members under which it is exact charge every set that the null charges, and it is then
     a numeraire [EVR26, Note 42]. Instance: for the Poisson family, `2 * 𝟙{x odd}` is admissible
     and a numeraire against no alternative.
   - **Extended numeraires.** With `c E = ⨆ μ ∈ effectiveSet S, ∫⁻ E ∂μ`, prove that `c E = 1`
     exactly when `E` is a limit of numeraires in the sense `∫⁻ E / X k ∂Q k → 1`, the alternatives
     `Q k` being `E • P k` normalized for members `P k`, and that every admissible e-variable has

   - **Dominated nulls.** For a null dominated by a σ-finite measure: a law
     `P̄ = ∑ i, 2^{-i} P i` of members whose null sets are the polar sets; admissibility through

6. **Nulls and bets** [BS99; LRR25, Definition 3.1].
   - **Operations on nulls.** Define `mapNull φ S := Measure.map φ '' S`, the image of a null by a
     kernel, the product `Set.image2 Measure.prod S T` of two nulls, `sampleNull S₁ n` (the laws
     `Measure.pi fun _ : Fin n ↦ P` for `P ∈ S₁`), `iidNull S₁` (the laws
     `Measure.infinitePi fun _ : ℕ ↦ P`) and `indepNull S₁` (the laws `Measure.infinitePi P` with

     and that its members are probability measures, or have mass at most one, when those of the
     argument do. Prove `iidNull S₁ ⊆ indepNull S₁`, that the image of `iidNull S₁` by the first `n`
     coordinates is `sampleNull S₁ n` for probability members, and
     `IsEVar X (mapNull φ S) ↔ IsEVar (X ∘ φ) S` for measurable `X` and `φ` (Statlib's `IsEVar.comp`
     is one direction), and the same for `IsPVar`.
   - **The polarity.** Define `polarNull 𝓔`, the measures `μ` with `∫⁻ X ∂μ ≤ 1` for every
     `X ∈ 𝓔`, with its σ-algebra as an instance argument (with an implicit one,
     `Convex ℝ≥0∞ (polarNull 𝓔)` does not elaborate), and the upper expectation
     `upperExp S X := ⨆ P ∈ S, ∫⁻ X ∂P`. For every null, prove the Galois connection
     `𝓔 ⊆ {X | IsEVar X S} ↔ S ⊆ polarNull 𝓔` for measurable `𝓔`,
     `IsEVar X S ↔ Measurable X ∧ upperExp S X ≤ 1`, and
     `effectiveSet S = polarNull {X | IsEVar X S}`, which holds by definition. Prove that every
     `polarNull 𝓔` is convex, solid and closed under setwise limits along any filter, with masses at
     most one when `1 ∈ 𝓔`, and that `{X | IsEVar X S}` is solid among measurable functions in the
     quasi-sure form (a measurable function below an e-variable quasi-surely is one; a
     non-measurable function is not). Convexity and closure under `liminf` are Statlib's
     `convex_isEVar` and `isEVar_liminf`.
   - **Quasi-surely.** Define `quasiSure S := ⨆ P ∈ S, ae P`, with `eventually_quasiSure_iff`, and
     the congruence and monotonicity lemmas of `IsEVar`, `IsPVar`, `IsCondEVar`,
     `IsTestSupermartingale` and `IsEProcess` with `=ᶠ[quasiSure S]` and `≤ᶠ[quasiSure S]`.

Generalization: arbitrary sets of measures, where [VW21] states calibrators for one probability
space at a time; `ℝ≥0∞`-valued p-variables, so that `E⁻¹` needs no truncation; validity under every
null, where the effective null and the bipolar theorem pair the integral with one.

## Layer 1: Optimal e-variables

Statlib provides the numeraire for any set of measures, any finite measure and any utility, its
uniqueness and optimality, and the duality
`maxUtility P S logUtility = ⨅ μ ∈ effectiveSet S, klIntegral P μ`. This layer identifies
numeraires, describes the effective null, and computes both sides of the duality for the classes
of nulls of the literature.

1. **The effective null** [LRR25, Definition 3.1, Lemma 4.5, Corollary 4.6; RW, §6.3.1,
   Theorem 6.10; BS99, Theorem 1.3; RLRR26, Corollary 3.6; EVR26, Notes 11, 40 and 48].
   - Statlib's `effectiveSet S` is `polarNull {X | IsEVar X S}` (Layer 0.6), and a closure
     operator: `subset_effectiveSet`, `effectiveSet_effectiveSet` and `isEVar_effectiveSet_iff`.
     Derive from the properties of polars that it is convex, solid
     (`μ ≤ ν ∈ effectiveSet S` gives `μ ∈ effectiveSet S`) and closed under setwise convergence,
     and prove that it contains every mixture `κ ∘ₘ π` of a measure `π` of mass at most one and a
     kernel `κ` with values in it, hence the convex hull of `S`.
   - **Divergences.** Define `klInf Q S := ⨅ P ∈ S, klIntegral Q P` in `EReal` and
     `maxEPower Q S := maxUtility Q S logUtility`. Prove: `klInf Q S = ⨅ P ∈ S, klDiv Q P` for a
     probability measure `Q` and a null of probability measures (`klInf_eq_iInf_klDiv`, through
     Statlib's `klIntegral_eq_klDiv`), and
     `klDiv μ ν = klIntegral μ ν + ν univ - μ univ` for finite measures with `μ ≪ ν` and an
     integrable log-likelihood ratio; nonnegativity of both for a probability measure `Q` and a
     null of masses at most one; the value zero of `maxEPower` under the probability measures of
     the effective null; data processing for both along `mapNull`; additivity of `maxEPower` on
     products of nulls; the simple null, `maxEPower Q {P} = klIntegral Q P` for `P` of mass at most
     one; and `maxEPower Q S ≤ klInf Q S` for a null of masses at most one. Statlib's duality
     `maxUtility_eq_iInf_klIntegral_effectiveSet` reads `maxEPower Q S = klInf Q (effectiveSet S)`.
     With `klDiv` in place of `klIntegral` both fail on sub-probability measures: for
     `S = {δ₀ / 2}` and `Q = δ₀`, `maxEPower Q S = log 2` and `klDiv Q (δ₀ / 2) = log 2 - 1 / 2`.
   - A probability measure `Q` admits, for some `α ∈ (0, 1)`, a level-`α` test `φ` for `S` with
     `α < ∫⁻ φ ∂Q` exactly when `Q ∉ effectiveSet S` [RW, Theorem 6.10]: through
     [RW, Fact 3.6], truncate an e-variable with `1 < ∫⁻ E ∂Q` (which exists by the definition of
     the effective null) to a bounded one.
   - For `S` dominated by a probability measure `μ`, the members of `effectiveSet S` are
     absolutely continuous with respect to `μ`, and `effectiveSet S` is the smallest set of
     measures that contains `S` and is convex, solid and closed under convergence in
     `μ`-probability of the densities [LRR25, Lemma 4.5]. The proof uses the bipolar theorem of
     Brannath and Schachermayer in `L⁰₊(μ)` [BS99, Theorem 1.3]; build it.
   - Corollary: for finite `S`, the probability measures of `effectiveSet S` are the convex hull
     of `S` [LRR25, Corollary 4.6], which gives [ZRW24, Theorem 3.4].
   - **The bipolar theorem on the side of functions** [BS99, Theorem 1.3]: for a probability
     measure `μ` and a set `𝓔` of measurable functions, a `μ`-almost everywhere finite `X` has
     `∫⁻ X ∂ν ≤ 1` for every σ-finite `ν ≪ μ` of `polarNull 𝓔` exactly when its class is in the
     smallest subset of `L⁰₊(μ)` that contains `𝓔` and is convex, solid and closed in
     `μ`-probability. With all of `polarNull 𝓔` in place of its dominated members the statement is
     false: for `𝓔 = {1}` the bets must then be at most one everywhere.
   - **No domination.** For any null on any measurable space, a measure is in `effectiveSet S`
     exactly when it is setwise below a member of the weak-* closed convex hull of `S` among the
     finitely additive measures of mass at most one; and the smallest relative entropy from a
     probability measure `Q` to the effective null equals the smallest one to that closure, the
     second minimum being attained at a finitely additive measure whose countably additive part
     dominates the reverse information projection [EVR26, Note 40; RLRR26, Corollary 3.6, for the
     equality of the minima].
   - Under domination, the effective null is the closure of the countable mixtures of `S` under
     limits in probability of the densities, made solid. Without domination this fails: a null on
     the countable ordinals, with the countable–co-countable σ-algebra, has a probability measure
     in its effective null that is singular to every integral mixture of its members; the
     construction uses an Ulam matrix and the axiom of choice [EVR26, Note 48].
   - **Bets on two observations.** On a finite alphabet, a function `H` of two observations is an
     e-variable for every i.i.d. law exactly when `J - H̄` is copositive, with `H̄` the
     symmetrization and `J` the matrix of ones; for a finite graph with clique number `ω ≥ 2`,
     `c * 𝟙{x₁ ~ x₂}` is an e-variable exactly when `c ≤ ω / (ω - 1)`. For `n` observations on any
     space, the e-variables of every i.i.d. law are the `H` with
     `M^{-n} ∑_{i ∈ [M]^n} H (y_{i_1}, …, y_{i_n}) ≤ 1` for every `M` and all points `y` [EVR26,
     Note 11].
   - Corollary: a null `S` such that every probability measure on `𝓧` is a limit in total variation
     of members of `S` has, for every finite number of i.i.d. observations, the e-variables of the
     null of all laws: `E` is an e-variable for `{Measure.pi (fun _ : ι ↦ P) | P ∈ S}` exactly when
     it is one for the products of all probability measures, by the closure under setwise
     convergence. Instance: the laws on `ℝ` with mean `0` and a finite positive variance are such
     a null, and its one-observation e-variables are the measurable functions bounded by `1`
     everywhere [EVR26, Note 22], the form for e-variables of the theorem of Bahadur and Savage
     [BS56]. Layer 6.2 states its consequences for power.
2. **Identifying numeraires** [LRR25, Theorem 4.1, Corollary 4.2, Theorem 4.7, Corollary 4.11;
   RW, Theorem 6.15, Corollary 6.16].
   - Say that `Q ≪ S` when every measurable set charged by `Q` is charged by some `P ∈ S`. For
     `Q ≪ S`, an e-variable `X*` for `S` that is `Q`-almost surely positive, and
     `P* = Q.withDensity X*⁻¹`, prove that `X*` is the numeraire of `S` under `Q` exactly when
     `P* ∈ effectiveSet S`, and that `P*` is then Statlib's `ripr Q S`, equivalent to `Q` [LRR25,
     Theorem 4.1]: the numeraire is the only e-variable that is a likelihood ratio against an
     equivalent element of the effective null [RW, Theorem 6.15]. Prove the criterion with a generating family of e-variables [LRR25,
     Corollary 4.2] and the dominated criterion `∫ p / p* ∂Q ≤ 1` for every `P ∈ S` [LRR25,
     Theorem 4.7].
   - For `S` convex and dominated by a probability measure, with `Q ≪ S` and `klDiv Q P < ∞` for
     some `P ∈ S`, every sequence `P n ∈ S` along which `klDiv Q (P n)` tends to the infimum has
     `log (p n) → log p*` in `L¹(Q)`, with `p*` the density of `ripr Q S`; a minimizer is the
     reverse information projection, and the infimum is `klDiv Q (ripr Q S)` [LRR25,
     Corollary 4.11, through the description gain of Corollary 4.10].
   - Derive as corollaries, with no separate proofs:
     - likelihood ratios: for `Q ≪ P`, `IsNumeraire (Q.rnDeriv P) {P} Q` with value `klDiv Q P`
       [RW, Theorem 3.20]; without absolute continuity the numeraire is `∞` on a `P`-null set `N`
       with `Q` restricted to `Nᶜ` absolutely continuous, and the likelihood ratio of
       `Q (· | Nᶜ)` to `P` on `Nᶜ` [LRR25, Corollary 3.15];
     - σ-algebras: the `𝒢`-measurable e-variables for `S` are the e-variables for the null
       restricted to `𝒢`, so Statlib's numeraire and the theorem applied on `(Ω, 𝒢)` give the
       log-optimal `𝒢`-measurable e-variable, `(Q.trim h𝒢).rnDeriv (P.trim h𝒢)` for a simple null
       when `Q.trim h𝒢 ≪ P.trim h𝒢` [RW, Proposition 3.22], the log-optimal calibrator of an
       exact p-variable `p`, `p ↦ E_P[dQ/dP | p]` when it is antitone [RW, Corollary 3.23], and,
       with
       `𝒢 = hτ.measurableSpace`, the log-optimal e-value at a stopping time `τ`, which is the
       likelihood ratio process at `τ` for a simple null [RW, Theorem 7.11] and the sequential
       reverse information projection for a composite null [KG22, Theorem 5];
     - families with a monotone likelihood ratio, whose likelihood ratio of `θ'` to `θ₀ ≤ θ'` is
       the numeraire of `{P θ | θ ≤ θ₀}` under `P θ'` [RW, Proposition 2.18], with Statlib's
       `isNumeraire_bernoulli` as the Bernoulli case;
     - the GROW theorem of [GdHK24, Theorem 1] for Bayes mixtures over a null, Li's definition of
       the reverse information projection, and [LGH24, Theorems 3 and 5].
   - The e-variables that are numeraires are characterized in Layer 0.5. For a null that lies
     between the products of members and the null of the product bet, the numeraire is the product
     of the numeraires [EVR26, Note 42].
   - For a null dominated by a σ-finite measure and every probability measure `Q`, `maxEPower Q S`
     is the infimum of the relative entropy from `Q` to the countable mixtures of members, with no
     other hypothesis [EVR26, Note 48]; [GdHK24, Theorem 1] (all priors, finiteness assumed) and
     [LRR25, Corollary 4.11] are cases.
   - **The conditional numeraire.** If `κ` is the conditional law given a statistic `T` under every
     member of `S`, `P.map T ≪ Q.map T` for every `P ∈ S`, and `κ ∘ₘ Q.map T` is in the effective
     null and dominates the probability measure `Q`, then `Q.rnDeriv (κ ∘ₘ Q.map T)` is the
     numeraire. Without `P.map T ≪ Q.map T` the statement is false, on a four-point space. The
     two-sample numeraires of Layer 1.5 are instances.
3. **Utilities beyond the logarithm** [LRR25, Theorem 6.1; Kon26, Corollary 6].
   - For the power utility $x\mapsto x^{1-\gamma}/(1-\gamma)$, $\gamma>1$ (a Statlib `Utility`),
     prove that the maximal expected utility is
     $\frac1{1-\gamma}\exp\big((1-\gamma)\inf_{P\in\mathrm{eff}(\mathcal P)}R_{1/\gamma}(Q\|P)\big)$,
     with Statlib's `renyiDiv`, and that the infimum is attained.
   - **Reverse Rényi projections.** For `Q` absolutely continuous with respect to the null, the
     optimal e-variables `E γ` for the power utilities converge to the numeraire `E*` as `γ ↓ 1`,
     with the bound `E_Q[χ(E γ / E*)] ≤ 2 E_Q|E*^{1-γ} - 1|`; the reverse Rényi projections converge
     to the reverse information projection in probability, and in total variation when the
     projection is a probability measure or the numeraire is bounded away from zero. The description
     gain of
     [LGH24] does not change from a σ-convex set to its closure, so the σ-convex and Choquet-convex
     hulls of [LGH24] give the same value, two conjectures of [LGH24] [EVR26, Note 27].
   - Extend Statlib's existence theorem `IsEVarClass.exists_max_eintegral_utility` to bounded
     concave upper semicontinuous utilities without a derivative, and deduce the composite
     Neyman–Pearson lemma [Kon26, Corollary 6] for any null and any `Q`, with `Qᵃ` the part of `Q`
     absolutely continuous with respect to the null: for `α ∈ (0, 1)`, an e-variable maximizing
     `∫⁻ min E α⁻¹ ∂Qᵃ` exists and can be chosen `[0, α⁻¹]`-valued; and a `[0, α⁻¹]`-valued
     e-variable is such a maximizer exactly when, for some `P* ∈ effectiveSet S` with
     `P* ≪ Qᵃ` and some `λ* ∈ [0, ∞)` with complementary slackness
     `λ* * (∫⁻ E ∂P* - 1) = 0`, it is `Qᵃ`-almost surely `α⁻¹` where
     `λ* * P*.rnDeriv Qᵃ < 1`, `0` where `λ* * P*.rnDeriv Qᵃ > 1`, and any value in `[0, α⁻¹]`
     where `λ* * P*.rnDeriv Qᵃ = 1`. The `[0, α⁻¹]`-valued
     e-variables are the level-`α` tests divided by `α` [RW, Fact 3.6], so this contains the
     classical lemma for a simple null.
4. **Nulls generated by constraints and the dual formula for $\mathrm{KL}_{\inf}$** [LRR26,
   Definition 2.1, Theorems 2.2, 3.1, 4.3, 5.5, 7.3, 7.6 and 9.2, Corollary 3.3, Lemma 7.2,
   Example 5.7; Lue69, Chapter 8].
   - For a set `Φ` of measurable real functions on a measurable space `𝓧`, define
     `constraintNull Φ`, the probability measures `μ` with `f` integrable and `∫ f ∂μ ≤ 0` for every
     `f ∈ Φ`. For finitely many constraints `g : ι → 𝓧 → ℝ`, `[Fintype ι]`, define the bets
     `Π g`, the `π : ι → ℝ≥0` with `0 ≤ 1 + ∑ i, π i * g i` quasi-surely for the null
     [LRR26, Corollary 3.3].
   - **Complete class.** For finite `Φ`, prove that a function `f` has `∫ f ∂μ ≤ 0` for every
     `μ ∈ constraintNull Φ` exactly when `f ≤ ∑ i, π i * g i` quasi-surely for some `π ≥ 0`, so
     that the e-variables are the functions quasi-surely dominated by a bet `1 + ∑ i, π i * g i`
     [LRR26, Theorem 3.1]; characterize the admissible ones [LRR26, Corollary 3.3], which contains
     the optimality of the dual class of properly constrained hypotheses [Cle24, Theorem 1]; prove
     that the relaxed null, with `∫ f⁺ < ∞` and `∫ f ∈ [-∞, 0]`, has the same e-variables [LRR26,
     Theorem 9.2]. On a standard Borel space, for a mean of a statistic `Φ : 𝓧 → ℝ^m` in any convex
     set `S`, the domination holds pointwise on the effective domain (the points charged by some
     member), by the affine e-variables `1 + λ · Φ - σ(λ)` with `σ` the support function of `S`
     restricted to the affine hull of the values of `Φ` [Cle26b, Proposition 1]; equality
     constraints are the case of a point `S`. For a set `𝓔₀` of measurable functions with finite
     values, `constraintNull {Y - 1 | Y ∈ 𝓔₀}` is the set of probability measures of
     `polarNull 𝓔₀` (finiteness cannot be dropped), and the complete class theorem reads: for a
     null presented by finitely many such bets `Y i`, the e-variables are the measurable functions
     quasi-surely below some `c + ∑ i, π i * Y i` with `π i ≥ 0` and `c + ∑ i, π i ≤ 1`, where `c`
     may be negative since the constraint on the mass is an equality. For arbitrary `Φ`, prove the
     description by the closure for $\sigma(L^\Phi,M^\Phi)$ of the cone generated by `Φ` minus the
     quasi-surely nonnegative functions [LRR26, Theorem 2.2].
   - **Admissible bets.** Every admissible e-variable (Layer 0.5) is quasi-surely equal to a bet,
     and a bet is admissible exactly when every bet whose combination `∑ i, π i * g i` is
     quasi-surely at least its own has a combination quasi-surely equal to its own; under the
     constraint qualification of [LRR26, Corollary 3.3] every bet is admissible. Prove that, with
     no constraint qualification, every e-variable is quasi-surely dominated by an admissible bet
     [EVR26, Note 04], so that the admissible bets form a minimal complete class [LRR26,
     Lemma 6.2]: [LRR26, Theorem 6.5] proves it under the constraint qualification, and [LRR26, §6]
     conjectures it in general. The proof works on a measurable set `Y` that carries every member
     of the null and on which the constraints admit no arbitrage (every nonnegative combination
     of the constraints that is nonnegative on `Y` vanishes on `Y`; LMLExplore's `NoArbitrageOn`).
     Every point of `Y` is an atom of a member of the null, so a property holds quasi-surely
     exactly when it holds on `Y`; the weights with a nonredundant support whose bet is
     nonnegative on `Y` form a compact set [LRR26, Lemma 7.2]; and among those that dominate a
     given e-variable on `Y`, a maximizer of the sum of the combinations at finitely many points
     of `Y` that detect the combinations vanishing on `Y` is admissible.
   - **Optimal bets.** For a Statlib `Utility` and a probability measure `Q` under which the
     `g i` are integrable, an optimal e-variable exists and is a bet with `π` in the compact set of
     [LRR26, Lemma 7.2] [LRR26, Theorem 7.3], unique under the condition of [LRR26, Theorem 7.6].
     For `logUtility` it is the numeraire, so that for `Q ≪ constraintNull Φ`,
     `maxUtility Q (constraintNull Φ) logUtility` is the supremum over `π ∈ Π g` of
     `∫ᵉ x, ENNReal.log (ENNReal.ofReal (1 + ∑ i, π i * g i x)) ∂Q`.
   - **Lagrange duality.** For a convex set `C` in a real vector space, a convex
     `F : C → ℝ` and convex `h : ι → C → ℝ` with finitely many indices, if some `x₀ ∈ C` has
     `h i x₀ < 0` for every `i` (Slater's condition) and the infimum below is finite, prove
     `⨅ x ∈ C, (∀ i, h i x ≤ 0) → F x = max_{π ≥ 0} ⨅ x ∈ C, F x + ∑ i, π i * h i x`, with the
     maximum attained [Lue69, Chapter 8], by separating in `ι → ℝ` with one extra coordinate
     (Mathlib's `geometric_hahn_banach_open`).
   - **The dual formula for $\mathrm{KL}_{\inf}$.** For finitely many measurable constraints and a
     probability measure `Q` under which every $\log^+|g_i|$ is integrable (Mathlib's
     `Real.posLog`), with no constraint qualification, prove
     $$\inf_{P\in\mathcal P_\Phi}\mathrm{KL}(Q,P)=\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_g}\mathbb E_Q\log\Big(1+\sum_i\pi_ig_i\Big)
     =\texttt{maxUtility Q (constraintNull Φ) logUtility}$$
     in `EReal` [EVR26, Note 20]. The null may be empty, and the three quantities may be `⊤`. The
     second equality needs no integrability and holds for every finite measure `Q`; when
     `maxUtility Q (constraintNull Φ) logUtility` is finite, the supremum is attained by a bet that
     is `Q`-almost surely the numeraire.
     - The supremum is at most `maxUtility`, which is at most the infimum, for every null: bets
       are e-variables, and Statlib's `maxUtility_singleton_eq_klDiv` with the monotonicity of
       `maxUtility` in the null.
     - For the converse inequalities, prove the alternative that the construction of the set `Y`
       of the admissible bets gives when it stops at the first removed set that `Q` charges:
       either a nonnegative combination of the constraints is nonnegative quasi-surely and
       `Q`-almost surely and positive on a set of positive `Q`-measure, or a set `Y` as above also
       carries `Q`. In the first case the multiples of the combination are bets with unbounded
       expected logarithms, and the three quantities are `⊤`.
     - In the second case every e-variable is at most a bet on `Y`, which gives the second
       equality and the attainment. For the first equality let `X*` be the numeraire. The measure
       with density $(1-\varepsilon)/(X^*(1+\delta\sum_i|g_i|))$ with respect to `Q` integrates
       the constraints and gives every e-variable a mean at most `1 - ε`. A finite measure on `Y`
       with these two properties becomes a member of the null once a finitely supported measure
       on `Y` is added: the complete class theorem, applied to the constraints shifted by
       multiples of their means, gives its points and weights. The relative entropy from `Q` to
       that member is at most
       $\texttt{maxUtility}+\mathbb E_Q\log(1+\delta\sum_i|g_i|)-\log(1-\varepsilon)$, and `δ`
       and `ε` tend to `0`.
     - Slater's condition (some probability measure `P` with `klDiv Q P < ∞`, the `g i`
       `P`-integrable and `∫ g i ∂P < 0`) implies the integrability of the $\log^+|g_i|$ under
       `Q`, by the Donsker–Varadhan inequality (Tau Ceti's
       `ofReal_integral_sub_log_integral_exp_le_klDiv`), and the finiteness of the three
       quantities: derive the formula under Slater's condition, with its maximum attained, as a
       corollary.
   - Derive as corollaries, with no separate proofs: Honda and Takemura's formula for laws on
     `[0, 1]` and on `(-∞, 1]` with one mean constraint [HT15, Proposition 1, Theorem 2], hence the
     $\mathrm K^\pm$ of Identification 1.4 for the laws on `[0, 1]`; the formula for a mean and a
     `(1 + ε)`-moment constraint on `ℝ` [AJK21, Lemma 7]; the portfolio nulls of [WSJ26,
     Lemma A.4]; Statlib's `maxUtility_bernoulli`. The complete class contains, for the laws of
     mean `m` on `[0, 1]` (the two constraints `x - m` and `m - x`), that the bets
     `1 + λ (x - m)` with `λ ∈ [-1/(1-m), 1/m]` are the maximal e-variables [Cle25, Theorem 1].
   - Equality constraints, encoded by `f` and `-f`, never satisfy Slater's condition: the dual
     formula covers them. Inequality constraints are the form of $\mathrm K^\pm$.
   - **Infinitely many constraints.** Sub-ψ nulls, the laws with
     $\int e^{\lambda x-\psi(\lambda)}\,d\mu\le1$ for $\lambda\in\Lambda$: every e-variable is
     dominated by a mixture $\int e^{\lambda x-\psi(\lambda)}\,\pi(d\lambda)$ [LRR26,

     null, the laws `μ` on `ℝ` with $\int e^{\lambda x-\lambda^2/2}\,d\mu\le1$ for every real

     for real `λ ≠ 0`, $k(0,x)=(x^2-1)/2$ and $k(\pm\infty,x)=-1$, prove that every nonnegative

     them [EVR26, Note 21]. Invariant nulls: for a compact

## Layer 2: Test supermartingales, optional stopping and the exact Ville identity

1. **Conditional e-variables, test supermartingales and wealth** [RW, Definitions 7.3(i) and
   7.19, Propositions 7.9 and 7.20; GdHK24, Definition 1, Proposition 2; WSR24, Proposition 3;
   WSJ26; ShSVV11].
   - Define `IsCondEVar X m S`: `X` is measurable and `∫⁻ ω in s, X ω ∂P ≤ P s` for every
     `P ∈ S` and every `m`-measurable `s`. Define `condNull m S`, the conditional laws `P[|s]` of
     the members given the `m`-measurable sets `s` with `P s ≠ 0`, and prove, for every null,
     `IsCondEVar X m S ↔ IsEVar X (condNull m S)`: on a set of infinite measure the conditional law
     is zero and both sides hold. The definition of `IsCondEVar` keeps its set-integral form.
   - Define `IsENNSupermartingale M ℱ S`: `M : ℕ → Ω → ℝ≥0∞` is adapted and
     `∫⁻ ω in s, M (n + 1) ω ∂P ≤ ∫⁻ ω in s, M n ω ∂P` for every `P ∈ S`, `n` and `ℱ n`-measurable
     `s`. Define `IsTestSupermartingale M ℱ S` as `IsENNSupermartingale M ℱ S` with
     `∫⁻ M 0 ∂P ≤ 1` for every `P ∈ S`, and `IsTestMartingale` with equality of the set
     integrals.
   - Bridges: a real nonnegative `Supermartingale` with `∫ M 0 ≤ 1` gives a test supermartingale
     through `ENNReal.ofReal`; a test supermartingale is almost surely finite, and its `toReal` is
     a real supermartingale (LMLExplore's `supermartingale_of_setLIntegral_succ_le`).
   - **Wealth of portfolios.** For a finite type `ι`, `ℱ (k + 1)`-measurable conditional
     e-variables `E k i` given `ℱ k`, and `ℱ k`-measurable portfolios `w k : ι → ℝ≥0∞` with
     `∑ i, w k i = 1` (the points of `LMLExplore.simplex ι` through `ENNReal.ofReal`), prove that
     `M 0 * ∏ k < n, ∑ i, w k i * E k i` is a test supermartingale for every `ℱ 0`-measurable
     e-variable `M 0`, and a test martingale when the `E k i` are exact. Derive, as the cases of
     one coordinate and of two coordinates one of which is `1`, the products of sequential
     e-variables [RW, Proposition 7.20; GdHK24, Proposition 2] and betting
     $\prod_k(1-\lambda_k+\lambda_kE_k)$ with $\lambda_k\in[0,1]$ [RW, Definition 7.21].
   - Conversely, every test supermartingale is `M 0` times the product of the conditional
     e-variables `M (k + 1) / M k`, with `0 / 0 = 0`, almost surely [WSR24, Proposition 3].
   - Mixtures `∫⁻ θ, M θ n ω ∂π` of a measurable family of test supermartingales over a
     probability measure `π` (Tonelli in `ℝ≥0∞`, with no integrability hypothesis); minima of two
     test supermartingales; stopped processes; and optional continuation: `M^A` stopped at `τ`
     times a process `M^B` equal to `1` up to `τ` [RW, Proposition 7.9].
2. **Convergence and optional stopping in `ℝ≥0∞`** [RW, Fact 7.5; ShSVV11, Theorem 1].
   - A test supermartingale converges almost surely to a limit `M ∞` that is almost surely
     finite, with `∫⁻ M ∞ ≤ ∫⁻ M 0`; derive it from Mathlib's
     `Submartingale.ae_tendsto_limitProcess` through the bridge of item 1.
   - Optional stopping: for stopping times `σ ≤ τ` with values in `WithTop ℕ`, prove
     `∫⁻ ω in s, stoppedValueLimsup M τ ω ∂P ≤ ∫⁻ ω in s, stoppedValueLimsup M σ ω ∂P` for every
     `s` measurable for `hσ.measurableSpace`; in particular `∫⁻ M τ ≤ 1`. Mathlib's
     `Submartingale.expected_stoppedValue_mono` is the bounded real case.
3. **The exact Ville identity and its consequences** [dlP+26, Theorems 1 and 4; Vil39; RW,
   Fact 7.6; WR23, Theorem 4.1; RM26, Theorem 4.1, Corollary 4.1.1; KPL25, Theorem 2.1].
   - **The identity.** For a test supermartingale `M` for `P` with `M 0 = 1`, a level `b > 1`, a
     horizon `N` and the first time `T` at which `b ≤ M n`, prove
     $1=b\,P(T\le N)+\mathbb E[(M_T-b)\mathbb 1\{T\le N\}]+\mathbb E\big[\sum_{k<T\wedge N}(M_k-\mathbb E[M_{k+1}\mid\mathcal F_k])\big]+\mathbb E[M_N\mathbb 1\{T>N\}]$
     (overshoot, predictable decrease and remainder), with Mathlib's `predictablePart` for the
     decrease [dlP+26, Theorem 1], and its form without horizon [dlP+26, Theorem 4].
   - Derive from it, with no separate proofs: Ville's inequality
     `P {ω | ∃ n, c ≤ M n ω} ≤ (∫⁻ ω, M 0 ω ∂P) / c` for `ℝ≥0∞`-valued test supermartingales,
     from which LMLExplore's `Supermartingale.measure_exists_le_le` follows through the bridge;
     its conditional form from any time `m`, for every `s` measurable for `ℱ m`,
     `P (s ∩ {∃ n ≥ m, c ≤ M n}) ≤ (∫⁻ ω in s, min (M m ω) c ∂P) / c` [WR23, Theorem 4.1]; and
     equality in Ville's inequality exactly when overshoot, decrease and remainder vanish.
   - The randomized form: for `U` uniform on `[0, 1]` and independent of `ℱ`, and a stopping time
     `τ`, `P (∃ n < τ, α⁻¹ ≤ M n, or U ≤ α * M τ) ≤ α` [RM26, Corollary 4.1.1].
   - The sharp bound for supermartingales bounded below by a nonincreasing curve crossing a
     nondecreasing curve, and its attainment [KPL25, Theorem 2.1]; Ville's inequality is the case
     of constant curves.
   - **Doob's maximal inequality with no integrability.** Define `IsENNSubmartingale M ℱ S` as
     `IsENNSupermartingale M ℱ S` (item 1) with the inequality between the set integrals
     reversed. For such an `M` under `P`, times `m ≤ N`, a level `C : ℝ≥0∞` and the event
     `A = {ω | ∃ n, m ≤ n ∧ n ≤ N ∧ C ≤ M n ω}`, prove
     `C * P (A ∩ G) ≤ ∫⁻ ω in A ∩ G, M N ω ∂P` for every `ℱ m`-measurable `G` [EVR26, Note 24].
     This bounds the conditional probability of `A` given `ℱ m` by the conditional mean of `M N`
     over `C`, and by `1`, and is informative when `M N` has an infinite mean: the form for
     submartingales of the extended Ville inequality above, a question of [WR23].

Generalization: `ℝ≥0∞`-valued processes with no integrability condition beyond the initial value,
so that products of e-variables with infinite values and nonintegrable bets are covered; any
filtration; an identity in place of Ville's inequality.

     Prove that `ν` is an outer measure, equal to `P` on measurable sets when `S = {P}`.
   - Prove [RLKR23, Theorem 3.7]: for `ε > 0` some e-process tends to `(ν A + ε)⁻¹` on `A`, and
     `ν A ≤ c⁻¹` when some e-process has supremum at least `c` on `A`. Derive [RLKR23,
     Theorem 3.1], `ν A = 0` exactly when some e-process tends to `∞` on `A`, by mixing the
     e-processes for `ε = 4⁻ᵏ` with weights `2⁻ᵏ`.
   - The truncated-Cauchy example [RLKR23, Example 2.5] has `sup P (A) = 0` and `ν A = 1` [RLKR23,
     Proposition 2.6].
   - Instance, a composite strong law: for the i.i.d. laws on `ℕ → ℝ` whose marginal `P₁` ranges
     over a set of integrable laws with $\int|x-\int y\,dP_1(y)|\,dP_1(x)\le c$ for one constant
     `c`, the set `A` of the sequences whose sample means do not converge in `ℝ` has `ν A = 0`:
     for every `α > 0` some stopping time of `Filtration.piLE` is finite on every sequence of `A`
     and finite with probability at most `α` under every member of the null [EVR26, Note 16]. The
     centred uniform integrability of [RLKR23, §4.2] implies the hypothesis.
6. **Fork-convexity** [RRLK22, Definitions 10–11, Lemma 12, Theorems 13–14, Corollary 16,
   Theorem 17; Del06; EVR26, Note 31].
   - Define the predicate that a null on a filtered space is closed under fork-convex combinations:
     for `P, P', P'' ∈ S` dominated by a reference measure, a time `n` and an `ℱ n`-measurable
     weight `h` with values in `[0, 1]`, the law that follows `P` up to time `n` and then the
     mixture, with weights `h` and `1 - h`, of the conditional laws of `P'` and `P''` given `ℱ n`,
     is in `S` [RRLK22, Definition 10]. With a set in place of the weight the predicate is stability
     under pasting, which does not give convexity. For closed convex dominated sets, [Del06]
     identifies fork-convexity with m-stability and rectangularity.
   - For a one-step null with convex values and observations with `X k` measurable for
     `ℱ (k + 1)`, the sequential null of Layer 4.5 is closed under fork-convex combinations, hence
     contains the fork-convex hull of `iidNull S₁`. Without the measurability of `X k` this fails,
     already for `S₁ = {Ber(1/2)}`.
   - Define fork-convex combinations and the closed fork-convex hull of a locally dominated null
     with respect to a reference measure, and prove that a process that is a nonnegative
     supermartingale for every member of `S` is one for every member of the closed fork-convex
     hull [RRLK22, Theorem 13].
   - For a locally dominated fork-convex null, the composite Snell envelope is the smallest process
     dominating `E` that is a nonnegative supermartingale for every member [RRLK22, Theorem 14];
     an e-process is dominated by a single process that is a supermartingale for every member
     exactly when it is an e-process for the closed fork-convex hull [RRLK22, Corollary 16].
   - For binary observations, the closed fork-convex hull of the i.i.d. Bernoulli laws is the set
     of all laws, so that every process that is a nonnegative supermartingale under all i.i.d.
     Bernoulli laws is nonincreasing [RRLK22, Theorem 17]. This is the standard example of
     e-processes (Layer 9.1) that no single test supermartingale dominates.
   - For a convex null every point of which is an atom of a member, the test supermartingales of
     the i.i.d. null are exactly the betting supermartingales, products of one-step e-variables
     chosen in the past, so they are the test supermartingales of the sequential null; for a null
     of mutually absolutely continuous laws, the i.i.d. null and its closed fork-convex hull
     contain the laws of independent observations with laws in the null; for all the coins, every
     test supermartingale is nonincreasing, while e-processes with power exist [EVR26, Note 31].
7. **Admissibility** [RRLK, Theorem 18, Proposition 9, Corollaries 26 and 29, Theorem 35].
   - Define admissibility as [RRLK, §4.2] does: `E'` dominates `E` when `P (E n ≤ E' n) = 1` for
     every `n` and `P ∈ S`, strictly when moreover `0 < P (E n < E' n)` for some `n` and `P ∈ S`;
     `E` is admissible when no e-process dominates it strictly. For a locally dominated null,
     every e-process is dominated by an admissible one [RRLK, Proposition 9], and every admissible
     e-process is the essential infimum over `P ∈ S` of `P`-martingales with initial expectation
     `1` [RRLK, Corollary 26]. Derive the simple-null case, where the admissible e-processes are the test
     martingales with initial expectation `1` [RRLK, Theorem 18]. Composite nonnegative
     martingales with initial expectation `1` are admissible [RRLK, Corollary 29].
   - An e-process is admissible exactly when, as a function on `ℕ × Ω` with `optionalSigma ℱ`, it
     is an admissible e-variable of `stoppedNull ℱ S` (item 1); with the criterion of Layer 0.5
     this gives a criterion for e-processes over the bounded stopping times [EVR26, Note 46].
   - On a discrete tree, the room at a node, `r v = ⨅ {s^P v | P ∈ S, 0 < P v}`, is the largest
     amount that can be added at `v`; an e-process is admissible exactly when the room is zero at
     every relevant node [EVR26, Note 31].
   - Instance: `exp (∑ k < n, X k - n / 2)` is admissible for the null of conditionally
     `1`-sub-Gaussian increments [RRLK, Theorem 35]. It is also admissible for the smaller null of
     the i.i.d. laws whose marginal is `1`-sub-Gaussian ($\int e^{\lambda x}\,d\mu\le
     e^{\lambda^2/2}$ for every real `λ`) and has no atom, with `Filtration.piLE` [EVR26,
     Note 02], as [RRLK] conjectures.

8. **E-processes that are optimal for a distribution over times** [KG22, Theorem 5; EVR26,
   Note 40].
   - For a null `S` of probability measures, a probability measure `Q` and a probability measure
     `ρ` on `ℕ`, prove that some e-process `E*` for `S` has `∑' n, ρ {n} * ∫⁻ E n / E* n ∂Q ≤ 1` for
     every e-process `E`; it maximizes the extended integral of `log (Function.uncurry E)` under the
     mixture `∑' n, ρ {n} • stoppedLaw ℱ Q n`, and the maximum is `klInf` from that mixture to the
     effective null of `stoppedNull ℱ S`. The proof applies Statlib's `exists_isNumeraire` and
     `maxUtility_eq_iInf_klIntegral_effectiveSet` on `ℕ × Ω` with `optionalSigma ℱ`, through the
     equivalence of item 1; no dominating measure is needed. For one stopping time `σ` in place of
     `ρ` the statement reduces to the numeraire of the null trimmed to the σ-algebra at `σ`, since
     `n ↦ Y * 𝟙{σ ≤ n}` is an e-process for every e-variable `Y` measurable at `σ`.
   - The identity of [KG22, Theorem 5], with the mixtures of the stopped laws of the null on the
     dual side, is false with finite mixtures on infinite spaces; it holds for dominated nulls with
     countable mixtures, from the bipolar theorem of Layer 1.1, and on every space with the closure
     of the mixtures among finitely additive measures, the countably additive part of a minimizer
     dominating the projection [EVR26, Note 40, Theorem 3].
   - Against an i.i.d. alternative whose one-step numeraire `X*` is finite, the product
     `∏ k < n, X* (ω k)` is a numeraire at every bounded stopping time, for every null that contains
     the products of members of `S₁` and for which it is an e-process; the proof uses Tonelli's
     theorem coordinate by coordinate and no measurable selection [EVR26, Note 40, Theorem 5]. For a
     simple i.i.d. null and an i.i.d. alternative with a singular part, no e-process is a numeraire

## Layer 4: Constructions of e-processes

1. **Likelihood ratio processes** [RW, §7.5; RRLK, Proposition 4].
   - For probability measures `P` and `Q` and a filtration `ℱ`, the process
     `n ↦ (Q.trim (ℱ.le n)).rnDeriv (P.trim (ℱ.le n))` is a test supermartingale for `P`, and a
     test martingale when `Q.trim (ℱ.le n) ≪ P.trim (ℱ.le n)` for every `n`. Its log-optimality at
     stopping times is Layer 1.2.
   - For i.i.d. observations and `Filtration.piLE` it is $\prod_{i\le n}\frac{dQ_1}{dP_1}(X_i)$
     (Statlib's `rnDeriv_trim_piLE_infinitePi`).
   - Conversely, on `ℕ → 𝓧` with `𝓧` standard Borel and the canonical filtration, every test
     martingale for a probability measure `P` with initial value `1` is the likelihood ratio
     process of some probability measure against `P`, built from the conditional densities
     `M (n + 1) / M n` with Mathlib's Ionescu–Tulcea `Kernel.traj` [RRLK, Proposition 4].
2. **Sequential predictors and their regret** [RW, §3.7, §6.6, §7.5, Definition 7.13,
   Proposition 7.14; HRMS21, Lemma 2; Rob70].
   - Let `X k` be `ℱ (k + 1)`-measurable observations in a measurable space `𝓧`, and let
     `κ k ω` be the conditional law of `X k` given `ℱ k` under `P`: for every `g : Ω → 𝓧 → ℝ≥0∞`
     measurable for `ℱ k` and `𝓧` jointly and every `ℱ k`-measurable `s`,
     `∫⁻ ω in s, g ω (X k ω) ∂P = ∫⁻ ω in s, ∫⁻ x, g ω x ∂(κ k ω) ∂P`. Let the prediction of
     round `k` have the jointly measurable density `r k ω` with respect to `κ k ω`, with
     `∫⁻ x, r k ω x ∂(κ k ω) ≤ 1`. Prove that $\prod_{k<n}r_k(X_k)$ is a test supermartingale
     for `P`, and a test martingale when the predictions are probability measures. For
     predictions given as `ℱ k`-measurable sub-Markov kernels on a countably generated `𝓧`, the
     density is Mathlib's `Kernel.rnDeriv`.
   - This is the restatement of `LMLExplore.predictorLikelihoodRatio` (Restatements of merged
     code): a finite alphabet with `κ k = ν` is the special case.
   - **Regret.** For a family of alternatives with conditional densities, the logarithm of the
     predictor's likelihood ratio equals that of every fixed alternative minus the log-loss regret
     of the predictor against it, pathwise. Deduce that a predictor whose expected regret against
     every alternative of a class is `o(n)` is asymptotically log-optimal against the class in the
     sense of [RW, Definition 7.13], and derive [RW, Proposition 7.14] (plug-in alternatives with
     log-concave likelihoods) by bounding the regret of the plug-in predictor.
   - **Mixtures.** For a kernel `θ ↦ Q θ` of alternatives with
     `(Q θ).trim (ℱ.le n) ≪ P.trim (ℱ.le n)` for every `θ` and `n`, and a prior `π`, the mixture
     over `π` of their likelihood ratio processes is the likelihood ratio process of the mixture,
     that of the predictor of the posterior predictive laws, so that Bayes factors with a simple
     null are test martingales [HRMS21, Lemma 2; Rob70], with the regret of the Gibbs mixtures of
     OnlineLearning 10. Without the absolute continuity the mixture is a test supermartingale. The
     Krichevsky–Trofimov and Laplace mixtures (LMLExplore's `InformationTheory/`) are instances.
     A Bayes factor with a prior on a composite null is in general not an e-variable for the null:
     give the example of [RW, §6.6].
3. **Universal inference** [WRB20, Theorems 1 and 9–11; RW, Theorem 5.1, Propositions 5.3 and
   7.18].
   - Let every member `P` of the null be locally dominated by `μ` (`P.trim (ℱ.le n) ≪
     μ.trim (ℱ.le n)` for every `n`), let `p̂ n` be `ℱ n`-measurable and `μ`-almost everywhere at
     least the density of every member on `ℱ n` (a null maximum likelihood), and, on
     `Ω = ℕ → 𝓧` with its canonical filtration, let `q n` be the density on `ℱ n` of the law of a
     sequential predictor, the trajectory `Kernel.traj` of its prediction kernels (item 2). Prove that `q n / p̂ n` is an
     e-process for `S`: it is dominated by the likelihood ratio process of the predictor against
     each member (Layers 3.2 and 4.1).
   - Derive the split likelihood ratio e-variable [WRB20, Theorem 1; RW, Theorem 5.1] as the case
     of one block fitted on independent data, averages over splits with their e-power [RW,
     Proposition 5.3], and the anytime-valid p-value and universal confidence sequence [WRB20,
     Theorems 10–11].
4. **Betting against conditional constraints** [Cle26b, Theorem 1, Proposition 2, Corollary 1;
   WSR24, Proposition 2; Cle25, Theorems 2–3; LRR26, §10; RW, §7.6, Algorithms 7.16–7.17].
   - For finitely many measurable `g : ι → 𝓧 → ℝ` and `ℱ (k + 1)`-measurable observations `X k`,
     define the sequential constraint null: the laws under which every `g i (X k)` is integrable
     and $\mathbb E[g_i(X_k)\mid\mathcal F_k]\le0$, in the set-integral form. Prove that the
     betting wealth $\prod_{k<n}(1+\sum_i\pi_{k,i}g_i(X_k))$, for `ℱ k`-measurable bets
     `π k ∈ Π g` (Layer 1.4), is a test supermartingale for every member, as a case of the
     portfolios of Layer 2.1.
   - **The complete class** [Cle26b, Theorem 1]. For a standard Borel space `𝓧`, a Borel statistic
     `Φ : 𝓧 → ℝ^m`, a nonempty convex `S ⊆ ℝ^m` (not necessarily closed or Borel), and
     `Ω = ℕ → 𝓧` with the filtration of the past observations, consider the null of the laws under
     which `Φ (X k)` is integrable and its conditional mean given the past lies in `S`. Prove that a
     nonnegative adapted process with `E 0 ≤ 1` is an e-process for this null exactly when it is
     majorized, pointwise and at every time, by a predictable product of the affine one-step
     e-variables of Layer 1.4 (Borel bets `λ k` of the past observations, the factor `∞` off the

     a one-step separation argument [Cle26b, Lemma 4].
   - Restricting the bets to those whose affine e-variables are maximal gives the minimal complete
     class [Cle26b, Proposition 2]; for equality constraints (`S` a point) every affine product is
     already maximal [Cle26b, Corollary 1].
   - The inequality constraints `g i ≤ 0` are the case `Φ = g`, `S = (-∞, 0]^ι`, with the bets
     `1 + ∑ i, π i * g i`. Derive, with no separate proofs: one mean on a Borel subset of `[0, 1]`

     many constraints.
   - The betting martingale of OnlineLearning 16.4 is the case of one mean constraint on `[0, 1]`
     with equality; the per-round log-optimal bets are the numeraires of Layer 1.4 for the
     conditional laws of the alternative (item 5).
   - **Infinitely many conditional constraints.** For the one-sided conditionally sub-Gaussian
     null on `ℕ → ℝ`, the laws with
     $\mathbb E[e^{\lambda X_k-\lambda^2/2}\mid X_0,\dots,X_{k-1}]\le1$ for every `k` and every
     `λ ≥ 0`, in the set-integral form, prove that every e-process for the filtration
     `Filtration.piLE` is dominated, at every time `n` and on every path, by a product
     $\prod_{k\le n}\int e^{\lambda X_k-\lambda^2/2}\,\pi_k(d\lambda)$ of mixtures of the
     exponentials, whose mixing measures `π k` on `[0, ∞)` have mass at most `1` and depend only on
     the observations before `k` [EVR26, Note 12]: the sequential form of [LRR26, Theorem 4.3],
     for the question of [Cle26b] and [LRR26, §10] on infinitely many constraints. The statement
     asserts no measurability of `π k` in the past observations, and uses the validity of the
     e-process only at bounded stopping times and under the finitely supported members of the
     null.
5. **Sequential nulls and sequential numeraires** [GdHK24, Proposition 2, Corollary 1; KG22,
   Theorem 12].
   - For one-step nulls `S₁ : ℕ → Ω → Set (Measure 𝓧)` that may depend on the round and on the

## Layer 6: Growth rates, power, expected stopping times and designs

The data are the coordinates of `ℕ → 𝓧` with the filtration `Filtration.piLT` (item 1), so that
time `n` has seen `n` observations; the alternative is `Q = Measure.infinitePi (fun _ ↦ Q₁)` and
the null contains `iidNull S₁`. Growth rates are per observation. Write
$\mathrm{KL}_{\inf}(Q_1,\mathcal P_1)$ for `klInf Q₁ S₁` (Layer 1.1), and

logarithm of an `n`-sample e-variable, which Statlib's duality writes as `klInf` from `Q₁^{⊗n}` to
the effective null of the `n`-sample null. Write
`d` for the limit of `a n / n` (item 1); they are LMLExplore's `maxLogGrowth Q₁ S₁ n` and
`bipolarRate Q₁ S₁`.

1. **The optimal growth rate** [RR26, Proposition 3.1, Lemmas 3.2–3.3, Theorem 3.5, Theorem 4.1,
   Propositions 4.5, 5.4 and 7.1].
   - **The clock.** Define `Filtration.piLT` on `ℕ → 𝓧`, LMLExplore's `Filtration.naturalLT` of the
     coordinates, prove `piLT (n + 1) = piLE n`, and transport e-processes and test
     supermartingales between the two filtrations by the shift.
   - Blockwise e-processes: for an increasing sequence of times `t k`, e-processes for the
     filtration `k ↦ ℱ (t k)`, lifted to `ℱ` [RR26, Definitions 2.3–2.5].
   - Prove that `a` is superadditive, from Statlib's `IsEVar.prod` [RR26, Lemma 3.2], so that
     `a n / n` converges to its supremum `d`; that every e-process and every blockwise e-process
     `W` has `limsup (1/n) ∫ᵉ ω, ENNReal.log (W n ω) ∂Q ≤ d` per observation, since `W n` is an
     e-variable of the observations it has seen [RR26,
     Lemma 3.3]; and that products over blocks of length `m` of Statlib's `numeraire` of the
     `m`-sample null attain every rate below `d` [RR26, Theorem 3.5]. No assumption is made on
     `S₁` or `Q₁`.
   - Prove `d ≤ KL_inf(Q₁, S₁)`, and `d = KL_inf(Q₁, S₁)` on any measurable space when
     `R ↦ KL_inf(R, S₁)` is lower semicontinuous at `Q₁`, in the sense of neighbourhoods, for the
     topology generated by the integrals of the bounded measurable functions: for every
     `c < KL_inf(Q₁, S₁)` there are finitely many bounded measurable `f i` and `ε > 0` such that
     every probability measure `R` with `|∫ f i ∂R - ∫ f i ∂Q₁| ≤ ε` for every `i` has
     `c ≤ KL_inf(R, S₁)` [EVR26, Note 15]. The proof bounds by `exp (-m c)`, under the `m`-fold
     product of each `P ∈ S₁`, the probability that the empirical averages of the `f i` are within
     `ε` of their means under `Q₁`, from the superadditivity of the relative entropy over
     coordinates, and turns that event into an `m`-sample e-variable. Derive the case of a Polish
     `𝓧` with `R ↦ KL_inf(R, S₁)` lower semicontinuous at `Q₁` for the weak topology, in
     particular a weakly compact `S₁` [RR26, Theorem 4.1]; then a test supermartingale on blocks
     has `(1/t k) log` tending to `KL_inf(Q₁, S₁)` almost surely and in expectation [RR26,
     Proposition 7.1]. For `S₁` weakly compact and convex, `a 1 = KL_inf(Q₁, S₁)` [RR26,
     Proposition 4.5]; and for any nonempty `S₁`, if `KL_inf(Q₁, S₁) ≤ a 1` then `a n = n * a 1`
     for every `n`, so that `d = KL_inf(Q₁, S₁)` [EVR26, Note 11].

     semicontinuity criterion above. On a finite type `𝓧`, `d = KL_inf(Q₁, S₁)` for every null of
     probability measures: `klDiv R P` is jointly lower semicontinuous and, for fixed `R`,
     continuous in `P` with values in `[0, ∞]`, so `KL_inf(R, S₁)` equals `KL_inf` of the closure of
     `S₁` and is lower semicontinuous in `R` by compactness, and the criterion applies; [RR26,
     Theorem 4.1] assumes a finite `KL_inf` and weak lower semicontinuity. A weakly closed null on
     `ℕ` can have `d ≤ log 2` and `KL_inf = ∞`: the laws `P j = ½ Q(· | ≠ j) + ½ δ_{j+1}` for
     `Q {x} = 2^{-(x+1)}`; the gap is a failure of lower semicontinuity, not of closedness.
   - On a countable type, `d` is the minimum of `∑' x, Q₁ {x} * log (Q₁ {x} / R {x})` over the
     pointwise limits `R` of members of `S₁`, which are sub-probability measures: `d` is `klInf`
     from `Q₁` to the pointwise closure of `S₁`; with probability measures only the corresponding
     statement is false. On any measurable space,
     `d = ⨆ k, ⨆ π, (1/k) * ⨅ P ∈ S₁, H_π (Q₁^k | P^k)` over the finite measurable partitions `π` of
     the `k`-fold product, with `H_π` the relative entropy of the restrictions to `π` [EVR26,
     Note 43].
   - For a null that is a finite union of closed convex pieces of a parametric family, a test
     supermartingale for the i.i.d. null is one for the i.i.d. null of the closed convex hull, so
     its tests need `log (1/α) / a 1` observations in expectation and have no power inside the
     hull; the minimum over the pieces of the likelihood ratios against the projections on the
     pieces is an e-process whose `(1/n) log` tends to `KL_inf`, so that `d = KL_inf` there [EVR26,
     Note 32].
   - The inequality `d ≤ KL_inf(Q₁, S₁)` can be strict [RR26, Proposition 5.4], and one
     observation may carry no evidence when `d = KL_inf(Q₁, S₁) > 0`. Instances on the circle
     `ℝ / ℤ`, with `Q₁` its Lebesgue measure and `ε ∈ (0, 1]`: for the null of the laws
     `(1 - ε) U + ε Q₁` with `U` uniform on a coset `x₀ + (1/N) ℤ`, `a 1 = 0` and
     `d = KL_inf(Q₁, S₁) = log (1/ε)`; for the null of the laws `(1 - ε) F + ε Q₁` with `F`
     finitely supported, `d = 0` and `KL_inf(Q₁, S₁) = log (1/ε)` [EVR26, Note 19].
   - For the sequential null of Layer 4.5, `a n = n * maxUtility Q₁ S₁ logUtility` (Statlib's
     `maxUtility_prod_eq_add`), and the product of the numeraires has
     `(1/n) log (E n) → maxUtility Q₁ S₁ logUtility` almost surely when
     $\mathbb E_Q|\log X^*(X_1)|<\infty$ (`strong_law_ae`). For a constraint null with every
     $\log^+|g_i|$ integrable under `Q₁` this rate is $\mathrm{KL}_{\inf}$ (Layer 1.4).
   - Almost surely under `Q`, every sequence of `n`-sample e-variables `M n` for the i.i.d. null,
     evaluated at the first `n` observations, in particular every e-process for the null, has
     `limsup (1/n) log (M n) ≤ d`; and the numeraires attain the rate: `(1/n) log` of Statlib's
     `numeraire` of the `n`-sample null under `Q₁^{⊗n}`, at the first `n` observations, tends to
     `d` almost surely, also when `d = ∞` [EVR26, Note 01]. No assumption is made on `S₁` or
     `Q₁`. An `n`-sample e-variable `G n` is compared with the numeraire `X* n` by Markov's
     inequality for `G n / X* n`, whose mean under `Q₁^{⊗n}` is at most `1`, and the
     Borel–Cantelli lemma; the lower bound uses the products of the numeraires of the blocks of
     length `m` and the strong law. Since `d ≤ KL_inf(Q₁, S₁)`, this contains
     `limsup (1/n) log (E n) ≤ KL_inf(Q₁, S₁)` [WSJ26, Proposition 2.2, for portfolio nulls].
   - The numeraires of the `n`-sample nulls, as a process, are not an e-process in general, a
     question of [RGVS23, §8.2.4]: for the null `{Ber(1/5), Ber(4/5)}` and `Q₁ = Ber(1/2)`, the
     stopping time that stops after one observation when it is `0` and after two otherwise gives
     them the mean `367/340` under the i.i.d. law of `Ber(1/5)`, while half of the process is an
     e-process. For a null `{P₁, P₂}` on a finite space, with `Q₁`, `P₁` and `P₂` charging every
     point, the numeraires form an e-process exactly when $\mathbb E_{Q_1}[p_2/p_1]\le1$ or
     $\mathbb E_{Q_1}[p_1/p_2]\le1$, that is, when they are products [EVR26, Note 23].
   - **A Stein lemma for composite nulls.** For `β ∈ (0, 1)` let
     $\alpha^*_n(\beta)=\inf\{\sup_{P\in\mathcal P_1}P^{\otimes n}(C):\ Q_1^{\otimes n}(C)\ge\beta\}$,
     over the measurable sets `C` of `n` observations. Prove
     $-\frac1n\log\alpha^*_n(\beta)\to d$ for every `β ∈ (0, 1)` [EVR26, Note 01]: the exponent
     does not depend on `β`, a strong converse. For a simple null it is the Chernoff–Stein lemma,
     with `d = klDiv Q₁ P₁`.
   - **One e-process for a class of alternatives.** For a countably generated `𝓧`, any null `S₁`
     and any s-finite measure `ρ` on `𝓧`, prove that one e-process for the null, nondecreasing in
     time, has `(1/n) log (E n)` tending to `d(Q₁)` almost surely under the i.i.d. law of `Q₁`,
     for every probability measure `Q₁ ≪ ρ` [EVR26, Note 13]. For a probability measure `ν` on
     the probability measures of `𝓧` that charges every Kullback–Leibler neighbourhood
     `{Q' | klDiv Q₁ Q' ≤ ε}` of `Q₁`, the numeraires of the `n`-sample nulls under the mixtures
     $\int Q'^{\otimes n}\,\nu(dQ')$ have `(1/n) log` tending to `d(Q₁)` almost surely under the
     i.i.d. law of `Q₁` [EVR26, Note 14]: the asymptotic log-optimality of the mixture numeraires
     that [RW, Chapter 6] conjectures, with the rate `d`.
   - **Block bets for a class of alternatives.** The lemma behind the previous bullet, as a
     statement of its own: for a countably generated `𝓧`, any null and a σ-finite `ρ`, there are
     bounded measurable `g i` on `𝓧^{m i}` with `∫ e^{g i} dP^{⊗ m i} ≤ 1` on the null and
     `⨆ i, (1 / m i) ∫ g i dQ^{⊗ m i} = d Q` for every probability measure `Q ≪ ρ` [EVR26,
     Note 13]. Item 3 and item 4 use it.
   - **Bahadur slopes.** For every sequence of p-variables `p n` of the `n`-sample nulls,
     `limsup (1/n) log (1 / p n) ≤ d` almost surely under `Q`, with convergence to `d` for the
     inverses of the numeraires, so that the best exact slope in Bahadur's sense is `2 d`.
2. **Power one** [Ram26, Theorem 3.1, Corollaries 3.2 and 3.6; RR26, Theorem 3.14, Lemma 3.9; RW,
   Proposition 3.12, Theorem 3.14].
   - Prove [Ram26, Theorem 3.1] for i.i.d. data on any measurable space, a null `S₁` and a set of
     alternatives `T₁`: the following are equivalent. For some (equivalently every) `α ∈ (0, 1)`,
     a level-`α` sequential test has power one against every alternative; some e-process for the
     null tends to `∞` almost surely under every alternative (or has `limsup` equal to `∞`); a
     countable family of finite-block events `B m ⊆ 𝓧^{n m}` has
     `sup P ∈ S₁, P^{⊗ n m} (B m) < Q₁^{⊗ n m} (B m)` for some `m`, for every alternative `Q₁`;
     the same with randomized fixed-sample tests, with bounded scores of nonpositive null
     expectation, or with bounded estimable functionals; and a test supermartingale on blocks
     tends to `∞` under every alternative.
   - The tests can be chosen with `E_Q[τ_α] ≤ A_Q + B_Q log(1/α)` [Ram26, Corollary 3.2], and for a
     simple null one-observation events suffice [Ram26, Corollary 3.6].
   - Derive [RR26, Theorem 3.14], the case of one alternative: power one holds exactly when
     `Q₁^{⊗k}` lies outside the effective null of the `k`-sample null for some `k`, exactly when
     `a k > 0` for some `k`, exactly when `d > 0` (finite-block events against the effective null,
     through Layer 1.1).
   - Instance without power [BS56]: for the null of the laws on `ℝ` with mean `0` and a finite
     positive variance (Layer 1.1), `a n = 0` for every `n` and `d = 0` against every `Q₁`; a
     measurable set of `n` observations with probability at most `α` under every i.i.d. law of
     the null has probability at most `α` under every i.i.d. law; and every e-process, for any
     filtration, for the laws of the independent sequences with marginals in the null is at most
     `1` at every time and on every sequence [EVR26, Note 22].
   - Test martingales [ZRW24, §7]: for a convex `S₁`, a probability measure `Q₁` with
     `Q₁ + ∑ i, b i • P i = ∑ i, c i • P i` for finitely many `P i ∈ S₁` and `b i, c i ≥ 0` (a
     member of the linear span of `S₁`), and a filtration each of whose σ-algebras depends on
     finitely many observations, every test martingale for the i.i.d. null with initial value `1`
     is a test martingale under the i.i.d. law of `Q₁`, so that none has power one against `Q₁`.
     Convexity is needed: for the null of the two point masses on a two-point space, some test
     martingale tends to `∞` almost surely under i.i.d. fair coin flips [EVR26, Note 03].
   - Derive the consistency of products of i.i.d. e-variables with positive e-power and their
     convergence to `0` with negative e-power [RW, Proposition 3.12]; and `1 < ∫⁻ X ∂Q` exactly
     when $\mathbb E_Q\log(1-\lambda+\lambda X)>0$ for some $\lambda\in[0,1]$ [RW,
     Theorem 3.14].
3. **Expected stopping times** [ARR26, Theorems 3.1 and 4.1; WSJ26, Theorems 2.3 and 3.3,
   Corollary 2.4; Gut09].
   - Lower bound: for every level-`α` sequential test `τ` for the null (`P (τ < ∞) ≤ α` for the
     i.i.d. laws of `S₁`), the expected number of observations satisfies
     $\mathbb E_Q[\tau]\ge\log(1/\alpha)/d$, with `d` the rate of item 1, for any `S₁` and
     `Q₁`; the expectation is infinite when `d = 0` [EVR26, Note 01]. This is the case
     `E n = α⁻¹ * 𝟙{τ ≤ n}` of a Wald inequality: for every e-process `E` for the null and every
     stopping time `τ` with values in `ℕ`,
     $\mathbb E_Q[\log E_\tau]\le d\,\mathbb E_Q[\tau]$ in `EReal`, with Statlib's `∫ᵉ` on the
     left [EVR26, Note 01]. Its proof restarts the e-process `k` times on fresh observations: the
     product of the `k` rounds, frozen at a time `N` close to `k` times the mean of `τ`, is an
     `N`-sample e-variable, whose expected logarithm is at most `a N ≤ N * d`. Since
     `d ≤ KL_inf(Q₁, S₁)`, this contains
     $\mathbb E_Q[\tau]\ge\log(1/\alpha)/\mathrm{KL}_{\inf}(Q_1,\mathcal P_1)$. For a family
     `(τ α)` of level-`α` tests, prove the almost sure form
     `liminf (τ (exp (-n)) / n) ≥ 1 / KL_inf(Q₁, S₁)` `Q`-almost surely [ARR26, Theorem 3.1].
   - The bound with `d` is attained to first order: for every real `d' > 0` strictly below `d`
     there are level-`α` tests `τ α`, `α ∈ (0, 1)`, with
     $\limsup_{\alpha\to0}\mathbb E_Q[\tau_\alpha]/\log(1/\alpha)\le1/d'$; they bet an
     `m`-sample e-variable on successive blocks of `m` observations and stop when the product
     reaches `α⁻¹` [EVR26, Note 01]. The bound with $\mathrm{KL}_{\inf}$ is not attained in
     general: for the null of [RR26, Proposition 5.4], $\mathrm{KL}_{\inf}>1/2$ and no power-one
     test exists (item 2).
   - **One test for every dominated alternative.** Under the hypotheses of the block bets of
     item 1, one nondecreasing e-process, built from those bets with the adjusted running maximum
     of [RW, Section 7.9], gives tests `τ α = inf {n | α⁻¹ ≤ E n}` with
     `E_Q[τ α] / log (1/α) → 1 / d Q` and `τ α / log (1/α) → 1 / d Q` almost surely, for every
     `Q ≪ ρ` with `0 < d Q < ∞`: the bound of the first bullet is attained in expectation at every
     dominated alternative at once. The proof uses Wald's identity for one block bet with values in
     a compact interval of `(0, ∞)` [EVR26, Note 13].
   - **First passage times.** For i.i.d. real increments with mean $D\in(0,\infty)$, the first time
     the partial sums reach `a` has expectation equivalent to `a / D` as `a → ∞` [Gut09]. Tau
     Ceti's elementary renewal theorem covers nonnegative increments; prove the signed case.
   - Upper bound: an e-process whose logarithm grows almost surely at the rate
     $\mathrm{KL}_{\inf}$, with the integrability of [ARR26, Theorem 4.1], attains the lower
     bound as `α → 0` [ARR26, Theorem 4.1]. Derive with first passage times the case of the
     product of the numeraires of Layer 4.5 when its rate (item 1) is $\mathrm{KL}_{\inf}$.
   - **Regret.** For portfolio e-processes (Layer 2.1) against i.i.d. vectors of e-variables with
     one coordinate identically `1` (the null class of [WSJ26, (2)]), the log-wealth is that of the
     best constant portfolio minus the regret, pathwise; portfolios with regret `o(n)` have
     `(1/n) log (W n) → ℓ*_Q` almost surely [WSJ26, Theorem 2.3] and
     $\mathbb E_Q[\tau_\alpha]/\log(1/\alpha)\to1/\ell^*_Q$ under the moment condition of
     [WSJ26, Theorem 3.3], where $\ell^*_Q=\mathrm{KL}_{\inf}$ by Layer 1.4. Cover's universal
     portfolio (OnlineLearning 11.4) is an instance [WSJ26, Corollary 2.4], and for one mean
     constraint on `[0, 1]` the universal portfolio with `k = 2` on the assets `1` and `X / m`,
     whose weights cover the bets in `[0, 1/m]`. The Krichevsky–Trofimov bettor (OnlineLearning
     13.2) is universal only for `{0, 1}`-valued observations: for constant coins `1/2` its regret
     against the best constant bet is linear.
   - Follow-the-leader bets [WSJ26, Remark 2.3]: for i.i.d. e-values `E k` of law `Q₁` on
     `[0, ∞)` and fractions `λ (n + 1) ∈ [0, 1]` that maximize
     `∑ k ≤ n, log (1 + λ * (E k - 1))` over `[0, 1]`, the wealth
     $\prod_{k<n}(1+\lambda_k(E_k-1))$ has `(1/n) log` tending almost surely to
     $\sup_{\lambda\in[0,1]}\mathbb E_{Q_1}\log(1+\lambda(E-1))$ when `Q₁ {0} = 0`, and vanishes
     at the second round with positive probability when `Q₁ {0} > 0` and `Q₁ (1, ∞) > 0`
     [EVR26, Note 07].

4. **Designs** [GK16, Theorem 1; KCG16, Lemma 1; EVR26, Notes 41 and 49]. Data collected by an
   algorithm that chooses which of finitely many sources to observe, as in the Conventions
   (adaptively collected data).
   - **Definitions.** An e-variable at a stopping time `τ` for a set `Λ` of instances is an
     `ℋ τ`-measurable `Y : Ω → ℝ≥0∞` with `∫⁻ Y * 𝟙{τ < ⊤} ∂P_λ ≤ 1` for every `λ ∈ Λ`, with `P_λ`
     the law of the run against `λ`. For `n : α → ℕ`, `a n` is the largest expected logarithm
     under `⊗ a, (μ a)^{⊗ n a}` of an e-variable for the null `{⊗ a, (λ a)^{⊗ n a} | λ ∈ Λ}`;
     `D* = ⨆ n ≠ 0, a n / ∑ a, n a`, and `D̄ x` is the growth of the designs proportional to
     `x : α → ℝ≥0`. Prove that `a` is monotone and that `a (m • r)` is the `m`-sample value of the
     null of blocks of shape `r`, so that `D* = ⨆ r ≠ 0, d (μ^(r), Λ^(r)) / |r|` with the rate `d`
     of item 1, and `D* = d` for one source.
   - **The Wald inequality with a design** [EVR26, Note 41]. If `E_μ[τ] < ∞`, then
     `∫ᵉ log Y ∂P_μ ≤ D̄ (E_μ[N τ]) ≤ D* * E_μ[τ]`, with `N τ` the vector of the numbers of
     observations of each source; if only `τ < ⊤` almost surely, the second bound still holds. The
     proof runs the algorithm on disjoint rectangles of a table of observations and compares with a
     design, as the proof of the Wald inequality of item 3, with the restart at a vector of counts.
     It contains the Wald inequality of item 3 (one source) and, through
     `a n ≤ ⨅ λ ∈ Λ, ∑ a, n a * klDiv (μ a) (λ a)`, the transportation lemma of Identification 2.2
     [KCG16, Lemma 1] for stopping times of finite mean, for e-variables rather than events.
   - **Consequences** [EVR26, Note 41]. A `δ`-correct algorithm (Identification 0.2) with
     `δ ≤ 1/2` that stops almost surely at `μ` has `E_μ[τ] ≥ kl(δ, 1-δ) / D*` and
     `kl(δ, 1-δ) ≤ D̄ (E_μ[N τ])`, with `D*` for `Λ` the alternatives of `μ`, and
     `D* ≤ 1 / T* μ`, so that this is the sharp form of [GK16, Theorem 1]. For testing a known
     instance, blocks of a near-optimal shape with the tests of item 3 attain `log (1/δ) / D*` to
     first order, and no sampling rule does better. If `δ`-correct algorithms attain `c` at `μ`,
     then `D* ≥ 1 / c`; with Identification 5.2 this gives `D* = 1 / T*` for best arm
     identification in exponential families.
   - **When `D*` is the entropy constant** [EVR26, Note 41]. On finite alphabets `D* = 1 / T* μ`,
     and `D̄ x = ⨅ λ ∈ Λ, ∑ a, x a * klDiv (μ a) (λ a)`, from the finite-alphabet equality of
     item 1; on countable alphabets `D*` is the same supremum with `klIntegral` in place of `klDiv`
     and the pointwise closure of `Λ`, from the countable-alphabet description of item 1.
   - **Instances with `D* < 1 / T*`** [EVR26, Note 41]: one source on `ℕ` against the null of
     item 1 with `d ≤ log 2` and `KL_inf = ∞`, where `T* = 0` and `D* = log 2`; two sources, that
     one and a Bernoulli one, where `D* ≈ 0.169` and `1 / T* = log (5/4) ≈ 0.223`. In such models
     no algorithm attains the entropy bound, and the algorithm of Identification 5.4 attains `D*`.

Generalization: arbitrary i.i.d. composite nulls with no domination, convexity or compactness, for
the growth rate and the existence of power-one tests [RR26], where [RW, Proposition 7.12] and
[WSJ26] treat a simple null and portfolio nulls; the rate `d` in the almost sure bound, the Stein
lemma and the bounds on expected stopping times, where [WSJ26] and [ARR26] have
$\mathrm{KL}_{\inf}$, which can be larger; the equality of `d` and $\mathrm{KL}_{\inf}$ on any
measurable space, where [RR26] assumes a Polish space; the Wald inequality with a design for any
observation spaces, any set of instances and the rate of blocks of observations, where [GK16]
and [KCG16] state relative entropies for exponential families or real rewards.

## Layer 7: Merging e-values

1. **E-merging functions** [Wan25, Theorem 1; Cle26; VW21, Proposition 3.1,

2. **Independent, co-valid and sequential e-values** [VW24, Proposition 1, Lemma 2, Theorems 1–3,

3. **Combining a p-value and an e-value** [IWR24, Theorem 3.1]. Prove that the four combiners

4. **Exchangeable e-values** [RM26; Wan25, Section 4 (d)]. For `ι = Fin K`, define the merging
   functions for exchangeable e-values as the e-variables of `mergingNull 𝒞` for `𝒞` the

   - Prove that a measurable `F` is one exactly when, for some `l ∈ [0, 1]`, its symmetrization

     It is not admissible: a monotone merging function for exchangeable e-values dominates it and
     differs from it [EVR26, Note 09], which answers the question of [Wan25, Section 4 (d)].

Generalization: merging for arbitrary nulls and spaces through laws, where [VW21] fixes a
probability space; non-symmetric and non-monotone merging functions [Wan25; Cle26].

## Layer 8: Multiple testing

**Setting.** Hypotheses are indexed by a type `ι`, with nulls `H : ι → Set (Measure Ω)` and the
set of true nulls `N P = {i | P ∈ H i}` under a probability measure `P`. A rejection set is a map
`R : Ω → Finset ι` with `{ω | i ∈ R ω}` measurable for every `i`; a candidate family is a map
`Ω → Set (Finset ι)`, from which any set may be selected after seeing the data. The false discovery
proportion of `R` is `#(R ∩ N) / max #R 1` in `ℝ≥0∞`. Guarantees hold for every probability
measure `P` on `Ω`, with no assumption on the dependence of the e-values.

1. **Dynamic e-closure** [dH26, Definitions 2.1–2.8, Theorems 3.6–3.7 and 3.10–3.12, Remark 3.8].
   - Hypotheses arrive over time: `I t` is the finite set of hypotheses active at time `t`, and a
     candidate family process `C t ⊆ 2^(I t)` is adapted. Define dynamic intersection e-process
     collections `E S`, an e-process for the intersection null of every nonempty finite `S`, their
     future-extension coherence (`E (S ∩ I t) t ≤ E S t`) and time monotonicity, simultaneous
     stopped FDR and simultaneous SupFDR, and the closure candidate family
     `{R ⊆ I t | ∀ S, FDP_S R ≤ α * E S t}`.
   - Prove the dynamic e-closure principle: for a coherent collection the closure controls
     simultaneous stopped FDR at every almost surely finite stopping time [dH26, Theorem 3.6]; for
     a coherent and time-monotone collection it controls simultaneous SupFDR and is setwise
     persistent [dH26, Theorem 3.7]; and conversely every candidate family process that controls
     simultaneous stopped FDR, or SupFDR, is contained in the closure of such a collection [dH26,
     Theorems 3.10–3.11]. Prove the same for bounded losses monotone in the true nulls and local
     in the rejection set [dH26, Theorem 3.12], which include the family-wise error.
   - **Fixed time and general losses** [XSF+26, Definitions 1, 2 and 5, Lemma 4, Theorem 6,
     Corollary 7, Theorems 12, 14 and 48]. For finitely many hypotheses at one time, a loss
     `f : Finset ι → Finset ι → ℝ≥0∞` with `f N ∅ = 0` and `f ∅ R = 0` (possibly unbounded, as the
     number of false discoveries), and an e-collection (`e : Finset ι → Ω → ℝ≥0∞`, `e ∅ = 0`,
     `∫⁻ e (N P) ∂P ≤ 1` for every `P`), prove [XSF+26, Theorem 6]: a candidate family controls `f`
     simultaneously at level `α` (`∫⁻ ⨆ R ∈ 𝓡 ω, f (N P) R ∂P ≤ α`, equivalently at every
     data-dependent choice [XSF+26, Lemma 4]) exactly when it is contained in
     `{R | ∀ S, f S R ≤ α * e S ω}` for some e-collection. For bounded monotone local losses it is
     the case of a single time and a fixed set of hypotheses of item 1 [dH26, Remark 3.8]. Derive the
     classical closure principle for the family-wise error rate [XSF+26, Theorem 12,
     Corollary 13], the FDR form [XSF+26, Theorem 14] and the post-hoc form at data-dependent
     levels, for an e-collection that does not depend on the level [XSF+26, Theorem 48].
2. **Compound e-values and e-BH** [IWR, Definition 2.1, Theorems 2.3, 4.3 and 4.4; WR22,
   Propositions 1–2, Theorem 2, §5.1; RW, Definitions 9.1, 9.8–9.9, Theorems 9.11 and 9.18–9.19,
   Proposition 9.16; XSF+26, Proposition 26].
   - An e-collection is a family indexed by the truth (Conventions): prove
     `IsECollection e H ↔ e ∅ = 0 ∧ ∀ N, IsEVar (e N) {P | IsProbabilityMeasure P ∧ trueNulls H P = N}`.
   - For finitely many hypotheses, `K = Fintype.card ι`, define `IsCompoundEVar E H`:
     `∑ i ∈ N P, ∫⁻ E i ∂P ≤ K` for every `P`. Unfolding the definitions, `S ↦ ∑ i ∈ S, E i / K`
     is an e-collection exactly when `E` is compound, and every self-consistent set `R` has
     `FDP_N R ≤ α * ∑ i ∈ N, E i / K`; e-variables for the `H i` and calibrated compound
     p-variables are compound [IWR, Theorem 2.3].
   - Derive from item 1, with no separate proofs: every self-consistent rejection set
     (`K ≤ α * #R * E i` for `i ∈ R`) has false discovery rate at most
     `(α / K) * ∑ i ∈ N P, ∫⁻ E i ∂P ≤ α`, with its post-hoc form over all levels [RW,
     Theorem 9.11; IWR, Theorem 4.3], and `α * #(N P) / K` for e-variables [WR22, Proposition 2];
     e-BH, with its threshold form [WR22, Proposition 1], the largest self-consistent set and the
     union of all of them [WR22, Theorem 2]; structured and post-selection e-BH [WR22, §5.1]; the Benjamini–Hochberg–
     Yekutieli procedure as e-BH applied to the calibrated p-variables `T (α / (ℓ_K * p i)) / α`
     [RW, Proposition 9.16].
   - Universality: every rejection procedure with false discovery rate at most `α` is e-BH applied
     to the compound e-variables `K * 𝟙{i ∈ R} / (α * max #R 1)` [IWR, Theorem 4.4; RW,
     Theorems 9.18–9.19].
   - **Adaptive e-BH** [EVR26, Note 33; MD26; IWR26]. For independent `Y i ∈ [0, 1]` with means at
     least `q`, `E[1 / (1 + ∑ i, Y i)] ≤ (1 - (1 - q)^{m+1}) / ((m + 1) q)`. Deduce that the
     e-values divided by a null-proportion estimate `π̂₀` are compound e-variables when the e-values
     of the true hypotheses are independent, so that every set self-consistent at the level
     `α / π̂₀`, in particular e-BH at that level, controls the false discovery rate at `α`; with a
     leave-one-out improvement, and the class of functions that can replace the estimate.
   - **Rounding e-values** [EVR26, Note 30; XR23]. The largest expected number of rejections of a
     rounding scheme of e-values is an explicit value `V x`, attained by a nested scheme; U-eBH is
     optimal among the comonotone schemes, which include the joint stochastic rounding of [XR23],
     and not among all.
3. **Closed e-BH, closed BH, closed testing for the family-wise error rate and online e-BH**
   [XSF+26, Theorem 17, Lemma 52; RW, Definition 9.31, Theorem 9.32; Goe26, Theorems 5, 8, 12
   and 13; HL25, Proposition 2.2, Theorems 2.3–2.5; VW21, Theorem 5.1; VW23; XFR26, Theorem 1;
   FXR26, Proposition 2.1, Theorem 2.2, Proposition 2.3; XR24, Theorem 1; WDR25, Theorem 2.4;
   FR25, Theorem 3.1].
   - Instances of item 1 with the mean e-collection `S ↦ ∑ i ∈ S, E i / #S`: closed e-BH, which
     controls the false discovery rate by [XSF+26, Theorem 14] and whose candidate sets contain
     every self-consistent set, so that it improves e-BH uniformly [XSF+26, Theorem 17 and its
     proof], with a largest set among the top-ranked hypotheses [XSF+26, Lemma 52];
     with the family-wise error loss, the e-closed test, strong control at data-dependent levels,
     at stopping times and uniformly in time [HL25, Proposition 2.2, Theorems 2.3–2.5], the
     family-wise valid adjustments of [VW21, Theorem 5.1] and the true-discovery bounds of [VW23].
   - Instances of the dynamic e-closure with fixed evidence (`I t = [t]`, the increasing
     e-collections of [XFR26]): the online SupFDR e-closure principle [XFR26, Theorem 1; dH26,
     Remark 3.8], online e-BH [FXR26, Proposition 2.1, Theorem 2.2] and e-LOND [FXR26,
     Proposition 2.3; XR24, Theorem 1]; with fixed hypotheses and evolving evidence, e-BH applied
     at a stopping time to e-processes for a common filtration [WDR25, Theorem 2.4].
   - E-BH applied to the running maxima `⨆ n, M i n` of test supermartingales for a common
     filtration does not control the false discovery rate at `α` [TGdH25]. With every hypothesis
     true, prove that it makes a rejection with probability at most `4 * α / 3` for two
     hypotheses and at most `3 * α / 2` for three, and that for every `K` and every
     `α ≤ (K + 1) / (2 * K)` some `K` test martingales for a common filtration make it reject
     with probability `2 * K * α / (K + 1)`, so that the two bounds are attained [EVR26, Notes 10
     and 18].
   - Closed BH [Goe26]: define PRDS (positive regression dependence on the subset of true nulls)
     and prove the lemma of Blanchard and Roquain that `E[𝟙{p i ≤ f p} / f p] ≤ 1` for valid
     p-variables under PRDS and coordinatewise nonincreasing `f` [Goe26, Theorem 5]; deduce that
     the e-collection built from the p-variables in [Goe26, (9)] is an e-collection under PRDS
     [Goe26, Theorem 8], so that its e-closure, closed BH, controls the false discovery rate
     [Goe26, Theorem 12] and never rejects fewer hypotheses than BH, with the level retained when
     the condition fails [Goe26, Theorem 13].
   - The online closed procedure SeqE-Guard for sequential e-variables controls the true
     discoveries uniformly in time [FR25, Theorem 3.1].
   - **BH at random times** [EVR26, Note 34; Su18, Theorems 2 and 3; FXR26]. For p-processes whose
     running infima satisfy the Simes inequality on the true hypotheses, the set that BH rejects
     at any time is self-consistent for the running infima, and at every random time `T`, a stopping
     time or not, `E[FDP T] ≤ E[sup t, FDP t] ≤ a (1 + log (1/a))` with `a = π₀ α`, and `≤ α` when
     every hypothesis is true; the clock instance shows that the bound is attained at a stopping
     time, and a time independent of the true hypotheses can lose the level.
4. **False coverage rate** [RW, Definitions 13.1 and 13.6, Theorem 13.7; XWR24]. Define
   e-confidence intervals `{θ | E θ < α⁻¹}`, level-free when `E` does not depend on `α`
   [RW, Definition 13.1], and the e-BY procedure [RW, Definition 13.6], and prove that for
   level-free e-confidence intervals it controls the false coverage rate of the selected intervals
   under arbitrary dependence and every selection rule, with its post-hoc form over all levels
   [RW, Theorem 13.7].

Generalization: hypotheses arriving over time with evidence evolving at arbitrary stopping times
[dH26], one null per hypothesis, each a set of measures, compound e-variables, and losses of any
form [XSF+26], where [WR22] treats e-variables and the false discovery rate at one time.

## Layer 9: Exchangeability, conformal e-prediction, change detection and two-sample testing

1. **Testing exchangeability** [LRR26, Theorem 5.5, Example 5.7; RRLK22, Theorems 6, 7 and 17;
   SR24, Theorem 2.1; Vov21].
   - At a fixed sample size, with Tau Ceti's `ExchangeableAt`: the e-variables for the exchangeable
     laws are those dominated by $1+f-\bar f$ with $f\ge-1$ and $\bar f\le0$ (Layer 1.4,
     [LRR26, Example 5.7]); the e-variables $f/\bar f$ of nonnegative $f$, with `0 / 0 = 1`, are
     the conformal and soft-rank e-values of item 2.
   - Sequentially, with Tau Ceti's `Exchangeable`: no process other than a nonincreasing one is a
     nonnegative supermartingale for all exchangeable laws of binary sequences in their natural
     filtration (Layer 3.6); the e-process of [RRLK22, Theorem 6] for exchangeability against
     Markov alternatives and its growth rate [RRLK22, Theorem 7]; the pairwise-betting test
     martingale in the filtration of pairs, and its growth rate for binary data under Markov
     alternatives [SR24, Theorem 2.1].
   - Conformal test martingales [Vov21]: the smoothed conformal p-values of an exchangeable
     sequence are i.i.d. uniform on `[0, 1]`, so that products of calibrated conformal p-values are
     test martingales in the filtration they generate.
2. **Conformal e-prediction** [Vov25, Propositions 2 and 11; BB24, Theorem 1; GBJ25, Theorem 2.5,
   Corollary 2.6, Proposition 3.3].
   - Define nonconformity e-measures (permutation-equivariant scores with average at most `1`, and
     their normalization) and the conformal e-predictor. Derive its validity for exchangeable data
     [Vov25, Proposition 2], the soft-rank e-value of exchangeable nonnegative scores [BB24,
     Theorem 1] and the validity of cross-conformal e-predictors [Vov25, Proposition 11] from
     item 1.
   - Prediction sets at data-dependent levels have post-hoc coverage (Layer 0.4) [GBJ25,
     Proposition 3.3], and products over batches exchangeable given the past batches are test
     supermartingales (Layer 2.1), with anytime-valid prediction sets [GBJ25, Theorem 2.5,
     Corollary 2.6].
3. **Change detection** [SRR24, Definitions 2.2 and 2.6, Theorem 2.4].
   - Define e-detectors for a pre-change null: adapted processes with
     `∫⁻ M τ ∂P ≤ ∫⁻ τ ∂P` for every stopping time `τ` and every pre-change `P`. Prove that the
     first time an e-detector reaches `α⁻¹` has expectation at least `α⁻¹` under every pre-change
     law [SRR24, Theorem 2.4].
   - The Shiryaev–Roberts and CUSUM e-detectors, sums and maxima over `j` of e-processes started
     at time `j`, are e-detectors [SRR24, Definition 2.6].
4. **Two-sample and invariance testing by betting** [SR23, Theorem 1, Corollary 1; PBKR23,
   Theorem 2].
   - For an involution `T` of `𝓧`, observations whose conditional law given the past is
     `T`-invariant under the null, and payoffs $g_k(Z_k)-g_k(TZ_k)$ with $g_k$ predictable in a
     symmetric class of functions with values in `[-1/2, 1/2]`, betting on the payoffs with
     predictable bets in `[-1, 1]` is a test martingale for every prediction strategy, as a case of
     Layer 4.4 with the constraints `±(g(z) - g(T z))` [SR23, Theorem 1(a), Corollary 1].
   - For i.i.d. observations under the alternative, prove power one when the prediction regret is
     `o(n)` and below the separation, and, under the assumption (10) of [SR23], the lower bound on
     the growth rate with the Online Newton Step bettor (OnlineLearning 8.3) [SR23, Theorem 1]. The two-sample test is
     the swap of the two coordinates, and the kernelized independence test of [PBKR23, Theorem 2]
     an instance.

Generalization: invariance under any involution, where [SR23] treats the two-sample problem and
symmetry separately.

## Completion checks

- Every definition comes with measurability, monotonicity in the null and congruence lemmas; for
  a singleton null `{P}`, `IsEVar`, `IsTestSupermartingale` and `IsEProcess` unfold to the
  classical notions for `P`.
- No result listed as a corollary is proved before the statement it is a corollary of; in
  particular the likelihood-ratio, monotone-likelihood-ratio and Bernoulli numeraires come from
  Layer 1.2, the bounded, semi-bounded and heavy-tailed $\mathrm K_{\inf}$ formulas from Layer 1.4,
  Ville's inequality from the exact Ville identity, and e-BH, closed e-BH, online e-BH and stopped
  e-BH from the e-closure principles of Layer 8.1.
- The validity of every construction of Layers 2.1, 4 and 9 is stated for every member of a
  composite null and every stopping time, and Ville's inequality of Layer 3.2 yields its level-`α`
  sequential test.
- The constant process `1` is a test martingale and Ville's inequality is sharp for it at `c = 1`;
  the e-process `1` gives the trivial confidence sequence `Set.univ`.
- The exponential supermartingales of Identification 4.2, the betting martingale of
  OnlineLearning 16.4, and LMLExplore's time-uniform bounds (`Probability/Martingale/MGFBound.lean`,
  `Freedman.lean`, `SelfNormalized.lean`, `InformationTheory/LaplaceMixture.lean`) are test
  supermartingales for the corresponding composite nulls and confidence sequences through
  Layer 5.2, with no restatement of their proofs.
- The dual formula of Layer 1.4 is proved with no constraint qualification, so that it holds for
  equality constraints; its form under Slater's condition is a corollary, and every admissible
  e-variable of Layer 1.4 is a bet.
- The rate `d` of Layer 6 is `klDiv Q₁ P₁` for a simple null `{P₁}`, for which the Stein lemma of
  Layer 6.1 is the Chernoff–Stein lemma with its strong converse; the bounds of Layers 6.1 and 6.3
  with $\mathrm{KL}_{\inf}$ are derived from the bounds with `d`.
- For a simple null and a simple alternative, Layer 6 gives the rate $\mathrm{KL}(Q_1,P_1)$ and
  $\mathbb E_Q[\tau_\alpha]\sim\log(1/\alpha)/\mathrm{KL}(Q_1,P_1)$ of the sequential probability
  ratio test; for one mean constraint on `[0, 1]` it gives the rate `klBer` on Bernoulli
  alternatives.
- e-BH with a single hypothesis is the e-test at level `α`; the e-Closure Principle with the
  family-wise error loss recovers the classical closure principle; closed BH rejects at least the
  hypotheses BH rejects.
- Coin betting's optimality [Cle25, Theorem 3] is the case of one mean constraint of Layer 4.4,
  and [RR26, Theorem 3.14] the case of one alternative of Layer 6.2.
- `LMLExplore.predictorLikelihoodRatio` and its three theorems are derived from Layer 4.2, and
  Statlib's `IsEVar.prod` is the product case of Layer 0.1.
- For a null of probability measures `klInf` is `⨅ P ∈ S, klDiv Q P`, and
  `maxEPower Q S = klInf Q (effectiveSet S)` is Statlib's duality; the effective null is
  `polarNull {X | IsEVar X S}` by definition.
- The closure properties of e-processes of Layer 3.2 that hold for e-variables follow from
  `IsEProcess E ℱ S ↔ IsEVar (Function.uncurry E) (stoppedNull ℱ S)` (Layer 3.1).
- The domination of e-merging functions by weighted means (Layer 7.1) is also a case of the complete
  class theorem of Layer 1.4.
- The Wald inequality of Layer 6.3 is the case of one source of Layer 6.4, and the transportation
  lemma of Identification 2.2, for stopping times of finite mean, and the lower bound of
  Identification 2.3 follow from Layer 6.4.

Compilation of the suggested signatures checks their types, not the truth of their admitted
theorems or the completion of these milestones.
